\chapter*{\centering{\Large Abstract}}

Gaze during visual search exhibits a rich spatiotemporal structure that offers a window into the neural computations underlying attention and perception. Active vision is an adaptive process in which information gathering is continuously guided by previous observations, engaging distributed neural circuits across the brain. However, existing models of gaze have largely remained divided between biological and predictive descriptions, and between the large-scale dynamics of saccades and the fine-scale movements occurring during fixations. In this thesis, we unify these perspectives into a single physics-inspired model, treating gaze as both a complex dynamical system and an adaptive probabilistic computation. Building on the theory of fractional neural sampling (FNS), we understand gaze trajectories through their dynamical signatures, which are demonstrated here: global superdiffusion across spatial scales and the nested oscillations of regular saccades, which modulate fine spatial sampling within fixations. This method presents a potential basis for non-invasive characterisation of neural function, with possible applications to disorders of attention and perception, especially ADHD and schizophrenia. Broadly, this framework presents a unified account of the rich spatiotemporal signatures of gaze dynamics, inspiring a mechanistic theory of neural computation with experimentally testable predictions.
