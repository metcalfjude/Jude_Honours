\chapter{Discussion}\label{chap:discussion}

% 1. Summary
In this thesis, we have developed a unified model of the rich multiscale dynamics of gaze during active visual search. Previous studies have largely addressed two distinct regimes of gaze behaviour: the relatively slow, large-scale movements associated with saccades, and the rapid, fine-scale dynamics occurring during fixations. Here, we bring these regimes together within a single physics-inspired theoretical framework. Moreover, previous approaches have often emphasised either the biological mechanisms underlying visual search or the development of generative models capable of reproducing empirical gaze data. Our approach seeks to bridge these perspectives by constructing a predictive model of gaze inspired by the cortical dynamics described by the theory of fractional neural sampling (FNS) \cite{qi_fractional_2022, chen_spatiotemporal_2022}, adding an information kernel to model saccade regularity and internal information state. We validated this model, developing methods to evaluate its predictions against human eye-tracking data.

% 2. The novel physics
From the perspective of the working statistical physicist, the dynamics examined in this thesis provide an experimentally accessible instance of a complex, nonequilibrium system exhibiting anomalous transport, nested oscillations, and history-dependent behaviour. Three principal features are of particular importance. First, visual dynamics are shown to be superdiffusive and to display robust monoscaling, governed by a single stability index $\alpha$. Such anomalous diffusion at all scales is of interest when studying how basic computational dynamics can be constructed from complex dynamical systems at criticality. We characterised the short-time diffusive scaling of eye-tracking data using fractional absolute moments (\cref{sec:search_superdiffusion}), and compared them with the monoscaling superdiffusion of Lévy dynamics (\cref{sec:levy_superdiffusion}). Such rich multiscale self-similarity provides a physical explanation arising from control by a neuronal network at criticality, with a diverging correlation length, resulting in nonlocal spatial access to an informative landscape \cite{Meshulam_coarse_2019, santo_scalefree_2018, Beggs_criciality_2022, tkacik_critical_2015, cocchi_critical_2017}. 

Second, the model presents a unique pattern of nested oscillations: the slow theta-scale rhythmic transitions between regions of interest (\cref{sec:theta_rhythmicity_of_saccades}) that modulate fast gamma-scale local sampling dynamics (\cref{sec:gamma_oscillations}), arising from patterns of neural computation governing gaze upstream in the brain. Nested timescales are a recurrent feature of coherent neural dynamics, especially during the communication of information between areas of the brain \cite{tan_theta_2024, spyropoulos_theta_2018, fries_mechanism_2005, bastos_communication_2015, fries_rhythms_2015, fries_rhythmic_2023}.

Third, the dynamical kernel introduced in this work describes how visual dynamics are influenced by the history of the gaze trajectory. In particular, the informational efficiency of gaze trajectories and the phenomenon of inhibition of return (IoR) suggest a strong dependence on the informative history of a search. Our formulation naturally encodes this information through a belief landscape that evolves from a prior saliency $\pi_0$ to a posterior belief $\pi_t$ after exploration. The cumulative information gathered is then well-defined by the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence. Such a well-motivated, physics-inspired development of interpretable memory effects, heavy-tailed noise, and nonlocality is important to the progress of physics as an interdisciplinary field.

% 3. Importance for neuroscience
This work advances neuroscience by turning gaze during active search into a paradigm of interpretable parameters that modulate neural attention, a very important topic for understanding decision-making in the brain. The active parameter space is defined here by the stability index $\alpha$, memory--momentum strength $\beta$, noise scale $\gamma$, and informative timescale $t_{\theta}$; each parameter has a well-understood and motivated interpretation in neural dynamics as gaze aggregates neural activity across a large region of the brain \cite{ZHAOPING2026103211}. Designing experimental paradigms to quantitatively summarise collective properties of brain function is important in developing methods for non-invasive neural measurement.

This work suggests experiments to apply this framework for understanding brain function. An experimental protocol that tracks high-frequency eye movements alongside coarse-grained saccade positions during a standardised search task without head restraint, combined with head acceleration measurements, would resolve gamma-scale fixational dynamics in naturalistic, head-free viewing and clarify the role of OMT during search. During such an experiment, coherence of the high-frequency sampling during fixations can be examined rigorously, as well as testing the saliency-frequency coupling described in \cref{subsec:saliency-frequency}, as well as performance in smooth pursuit experiments and mapping parameter-space dynamics as described in \cref{subsec:smooth_pursuit_and_predictions}. Such an experiment is simple to design using low-cost, non-invasive techniques, as described in \cite{ryle_simultaneous_2015}, and the extracted data are amenable to analysis across the scales of interest here to test the predictions of FNS dynamics presented here.

% 4. Importance and application to machine learning
The same sampling process applies to machine learning, where MCMC draws samples from a posterior $\pi_{\text{post}}(\bm{\theta})$ by proposing local moves through the landscape $V(\bm{\theta}) = -\ln \pi_{\text{post}}(\bm{\theta})$, often with momentum-coupled Langevin dynamics (\cref{sec:diffusive_processes}). Such samplers struggle to mix between well-separated modes without hand-tuned hyperparameters \cite{neal_sampling_1996, latuszynski_mcmc_2025, speagle_conceptual_2020}. As outlined in \cref{subsec:superdiffusive_probability_sampling}, each component of our model targets part of this problem. Superdiffusive jumps give direct access to distant modes. Momentum-driven oscillations decorrelate local samples, as in Hamiltonian Monte Carlo \cite{betancourt_conceptual_2018, Ottobre2016-xp} and second-order Langevin sampling \cite{liu2025secondorderensemblelangevin, Ottobre2016-xp}. The adaptive kernel resembles well-tempered metadynamics \cite{tiwary_time-independent_2015}, discouraging revisits without tuning to the unknown landscape. Whether coupling fast local oscillations to slower rhythmic transitions benefits a sampler remains to be tested.

Superdiffusive sampling is an active area of MCMC research \cite{simsekli_fractional_nodate, ijcai2018p419, latuszynski_mcmc_2025, oechsler_levy_2024, behme_levy_2026}. The same heavy-tailed nonlocality underlies our group's Fractional Neural Attention (FNA) \cite{qu2025fractionalneuralattentionefficient}, which models token interactions with the fractional Laplacian and matches or exceeds Transformer baselines on text classification, image classification and machine translation. That one mechanism appears in both gaze and artificial attention is consistent with multiscale, nonlocal sampling being a general computational strategy. Formalising the link to free-energy minimisation and active inference \cite{NIPS2017_194cf6c2, tiwary_time-independent_2015, bussi_using_2020, Friston2010-aa, parr_active_2022} is a natural next step, since both treat search as the minimisation of uncertainty about a landscape.


% 5. Broad conclusions
In conclusion, this thesis demonstrates that the apparently disparate dynamics of human gaze can be described within a unified physical framework comprising a small number of interpretable mechanisms: nonlocal Lévy motion, nested oscillatory dynamics, and an adaptive information-dependent sampling landscape. By connecting these mechanisms to the theory of fractional neural sampling (FNS), the model offers a quantitative account of how complex neural dynamics may give rise to the coordinated exploration of visual information across spatial and temporal scales. As a readily observable behavioural proxy for attention, gaze provides a valuable window into aspects of neural decision-making. The framework developed here establishes testable predictions for future experiments, provides new opportunities for the non-invasive characterisation of neural function, and suggests broader applications of multiscale stochastic dynamics to probabilistic computation and artificial intelligence.
