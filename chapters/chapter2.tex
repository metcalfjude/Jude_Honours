\chapter{A Unifying Model}\label{chap:a_unifying_model}
Eye movements during visual search look like a patchwork of distinct behaviours: nonlocal jumps (saccades), periods of fixation, and fine motion within fixations. The aim of this chapter is to show that these can arise from a single physical picture, and to motivate each dynamical component from first principles rather than treating each scale separately. We model gaze dynamics as a stochastic process driven by travelling waves of neural activity, using the theory of fractional neural sampling (FNS) \cite{qi_fractional_2022, chen_spatiotemporal_2022}. Each ingredient of the model has a distinct physical origin. Nonlocal dynamic produce heavy-tailed jumps and Lévy superdiffusion, giving global access to the scene without traversing the space in between. A localised confining potential around an informative region produces high-frequency harmonic oscillations during fixation. Finally, because the scene being searched is not static, we introduce a dynamical-informative kernel: gaze deposits onto the scene, saturating regions it has explored and driving transitions between regions of interest with a regular temporal structure. Together, these three components give a minimal description of the dynamics needed to search a visual scene.

The chapter is organised as follows. \Cref{sec:model_setup} defines the FNS model and the search task used as the paradigm throughout this work. \Cref{sec:dynamical-information_adaptation} introduces the dynamical-informative kernel, giving its motivation (\cref{subsec:motivations}) and the regular temporal structure of transitions between regions of interest as they are explored and saturated through kernel deposition (\cref{subsec:adaptive_kernel_dynamics}). \Cref{sec:local_harmonic_oscillations} derives and quantifies the prediction that localised trajectories produce high-frequency simple harmonic oscillations. \Cref{sec:levy_superdiffusion} treats the defining characteristic of FNS, Lévy superdiffusion, deriving the short-time, noise-driven scaling of fractional absolute moments and the limits of monofractality that the model can represent. Comparison of each of these dynamics with empirical data is deferred to the next chapter.
