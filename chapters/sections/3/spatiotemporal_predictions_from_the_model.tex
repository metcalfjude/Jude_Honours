\section{Spatiotemporal predictions from the model}\label{sec:spatiotemporal_predictions}

Throughout this chapter, we present a novel physics-inspired analysis of a standard gaze-tracking experiment during visual search, offering an interpretation of the rich spatiotemporal behaviour of gaze dynamics. In particular, the approximate timescale of saccades has been known since the inception of eye movement research. In contrast, the significance of the rate-corrected local rhythmicity is a very recent result, only discovered in the year of this present work. OMT, as a phenomenon, has been documented in the literature \cite{graham_ocular_2023}; however, no consensus exists on its importance or functional role in gaze dynamics, nor has it been linked to cortical gamma. Lastly, the mathematical analysis of gaze search superdiffusion provides a well-motivated link to the fractional model of gaze we present. Comparing the unifying framework of this thesis with the data in \cref{chap:empirical_validations} admits dynamical predictions beyond the present literature. We present features of this model being developed to better examine neurological function through visual search in an experiment.
\subsection{Saliency-frequency coupling in contrast-dependent
search tasks}\label{subsec:saliency-frequency}
OMT frequency is hypothesised here to result from harmonic oscillations in the neural control of the eye's path during fixation. An experiment to examine this prediction may be designed in which targets of different contrast are presented in a search task. High-contrast, localised points, such as tight, high-spatial-frequency Gabor patches, are modelled with localised saliency wells with low $\sigma_{\text{fix}}$. Whereas, low contrast, large spatial frequency patches would produce saliency wells with a large width $\sigma_{\text{fix}}$. High contrast wells correspond to longer theta timescales, with $t_{\theta}$ inverse quadratic in the effective saliency well size as shown in \cref{eq:theta_rhythm}. The corresponding OMT peak frequency is then inversely proportional to the effective saliency well size, as in \cref{eq:resonant_frequency}. The effect of visual contrast has been found significant in cortical gamma frequency in the visual system \cite{le_gamma_2019, baranauskas_spatial_2016, han_gamma_2022, RAY2010885}, and this quantitative relationship provides an experimental prediction of the relationship between three data points: $t_{\theta}, f_{\text{peak}},$ and $k_{\text{Gabor}}$. A regression between these variables can be tested against the model predictions: $t_{\theta} \propto \sigma_{\text{fix}}^{-2}$ and $f \propto \sigma_{\text{fix}}^{-1}$.
\subsection{Smooth pursuit predictions and parametric-fitting techniques}\label{subsec:smooth_pursuit_and_predictions}

Another experimental paradigm that provides insight into the behaviour of visual trajectory control. In particular, this model can be tested in the smooth pursuit paradigm, which involves tracking a moving object \cite{erkelens_coordination_2006, yan_metrics_2025}. Real pursuit is not perfectly smooth; it contains microsaccades and catch-up saccades. Lévy noise and the kernel deposition govern the strength and statistics of these microsaccades and smooth pursuit. The model predicts that saccades cluster where motion is slow, as kernel deposition is strong under a slow-moving target. ``Catch-up'' saccade rate rises, and pursuit gain falls as the target period approaches the theta-rhythmic saccade timescale $t_\theta$. Using such a smooth pursuit experiment, a subject could be fitted based on the statistics of gaze to some subspace of $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma, \sigma_{\text{fix}}, t_\theta)$. This could then be correlated with neurological function. For instance, schizophrenia is well known to impair smooth pursuit, which permits statistical analysis and parametric fitting techniques to study neurological disorder \cite{caldani_saccadic_2017, law_eye-tracking_1973, morita_eye_2017, zhu_diagnosis_2024}. Group differences can then be fit directly in parameter space. See \cref{subsec:dynamical_goals} for a similar discussion in the context of abstract sampling.
