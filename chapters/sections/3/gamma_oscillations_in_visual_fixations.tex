\section{Gamma oscillations during visual fixation}\label{sec:gamma_oscillations}
We next verify the short-time and fine spatial scales of our model predictions, specifically of those high frequencies found in local sampling of potential modes in \cref{sec:local_harmonic_oscillations}. Typically short-time gaze dynamics are divided into microsaccades, low-frequency linear drift, and high frequency ocular tremor \cite{rucci_control_2015}. There is increasing evidence that microsaccades---which is the name given to saccades which do not interrupt fixation---are driven by the same statistics as regular (called macro-) saccades. Unifying and treating these microsaccades as unified with global macrosaccades is performed in our analysis of superdiffusion in the next section (\cref{sec:search_superdiffusion}). The next component of fixation, linear drifts have been found to be important in gathering information. An account of low-frequency linear drift fits into the model presented here, through similar local self-avoidance arising from kernel dynamics described in \cref{subsec:existing_models} \cite{engbert_integrated_2011}. 

But our focus here is that of the high frequency motion called ocular microtremor (OMT) found in fixation \cite{graham_ocular_2023, higgins_frequency_1953, bolger_dominant_1999} and often treated as muscular noise \cite{roberts_fixational_2013, greschner_retinal_2002, graham_ocular_2023}. In this thesis, we make the well-motivated prediction from our unified framework that OMT is the gamma-scale sampling pattern driven by neural gamma in visual search. In this section, we describe how OMT has previously been characterised (\cref{subsec:previous_treatment_of_OMT}), comparisons of our modelling framework of neural gamma to the data (\cref{subsec:fixational_spectra}), and briefly discuss their implications for the study of OMT. 
\subsection{Previous treatment of ocular microtremor (OMT)}\label{subsec:previous_treatment_of_OMT}

OMT is the smallest-amplitude component of fixational eye motion. It has been observed across experimental studies, with reported amplitudes of up to \qtyrange{30}{40}{\sec} of arc \cite{ryle_simultaneous_2015, alkalbani2010thesis}. Despite its small amplitude, OMT has been proposed to contribute to the dynamic sampling of high-contrast features and fine spatial detail \cite{rucci_miniature_2007, rucci_temporal_2018, rucci_unsteady_2015, roberts_fixational_2013}. However, a recent physical study concluded that its magnitude may be insufficient to produce a meaningful change in the retinal image during prolonged fixation \cite{bowers_effects_2019}. We therefore consider our results under the hypothesis that the role of microtremor will be task-dependant: it may play an important functional role in controlling gaze during visual search, when gaze is rapidly adjusted in response to incoming information, than during the prolonged fixation tasks where OMT is usually studied \cite{ko_eye_2016, alkalbani2010thesis, ryle_simultaneous_2015}. Further, the frequency and amplitude of OMT has been demonstrated to correlate with broader brain function and state, such as being a biomarker for neurological fatigue \cite{lyapunov2022fatigue}, anaesthesia \cite{bojanic2001anaesthesia, heaney2004anesthesia, kevin2002bis}, and schizophrenia \cite{shoshina2023procedia}.

In our search-task data, the tremor spectrum has a broad peak at a lower frequency than is typically reported. Our results yield a peak at \qty{56}{\hertz}, and with a larger estimated amplitude. By comparison, studies of longer fixation periods generally report more localised peaks around \qtyrange{80}{90}{\hertz} \cite{graham_ocular_2023}. This reiterates the result that OMT is task-dependant and provides an external summary of computation in the visual system. In gathering our results, the present analysis differs from the existing literature in two important respects:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We examine eye movements during a naturalistic visual search task. The major studies which report OMT, as surveyed in the review by \textcite{graham_ocular_2023}, instead measure tremor across a prolonged fixation, typically lasting more than \qty{3000}{\milli \second} \cite{ko_eye_2016, alkalbani2010thesis, ryle_simultaneous_2015}.
    \item The data considered here are positional, not accelerometer or direct velocity data. OMT is studied using velocity or acceleration, as these data effectively ignore the low frequency drifts and are more sensitive to OMT amplitude. In our data, the estimated tremor amplitude is larger than values reported during prolonged fixation: approximately \qty{200}{\sec} of arc, compared with values of up to \qty{40}{\sec} of arc in prior studies \cite{ryle_simultaneous_2015, alkalbani2010thesis} (see \cref{app:decomposition} for the decomposition of a typical fixation into Fourier components and their residual). 
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Analysis of fixational gaze velocity}
\label{subsec:fixational_spectra}
We analyse the profile of high frequency components of fixational gaze trajectories during visual search through their velocities. The shorter, naturalistic searching fixations in our data pose a challenge for estimating high-frequency motion. In our data, fixations have an average duration of approximately \qty{200}{\milli\second}, which will yields only ten cycles of a \qty{50}{\hertz} signal. Moreover, estimating high-frequency motion from noisy position measurements can be misleading, as the low frequencies dominate the positional signal power of a fixation \cite{ko_eye_2016, ryle_simultaneous_2015}. This motivates our data pre-processing to low-pass filter the instrumental noise, and numerically differentiate to consider the velocity instead. Such a method has been used elsewhere to analyse OMT, with signal amplitude found within instrument error \cite{ko_eye_2016}.

% Our recordings were sampled at \qty{500}{\hertz}, with reported positional precision of up to \qty{36}{\sec} of arc \cite{srresearcheyelink1000specs}. Because low-frequency drift can dominate the positional power spectrum, we analyse velocity rather than position. The expectation of the position spectrum is that it will scale approximately as $\mathrm{PSD}_{\mathbf{x}}(f) \sim f^{-2}$, which is the assumption for tremor-less gaze fixations modelled with Brownian noise \cite{engbert_normal, engbert_integrated_2011}. Velocity, as found through differentiation will multiply spectral power by a factor by  $(2 \pi f)^2$, yielding an approximately flat velocity spectrum with no peak. Thus, under a baseline model with no distinct tremor component, we would expect no peak in the velocity spectrum, instead just a flat baseline with $\mathrm{PSD}_{\mathbf{v}}(f) \sim f^{0}$.

\subsubsection*{Data processing}
The analysis pipeline was identical for the human eye-tracking data and the model gaze trajectories unless otherwise stated. Fixations were taken from the existing labels in the eye-tracking data or, where unlabelled, detected with a variant of the median-filter method of \cite{Schweitzer2020-nu, ENGBERT20031035}, manually verified and implemented in \texttt{VisualFNS\_Analysis.jl} (\cref{app:code}). Fixations were truncated at the first NaN sample, and those shorter than \qty{100}{\milli\second} were discarded. To suppress high-frequency instrumental noise near the Nyquist frequency ($f_{\text{Nyquist}}=\qty{250}{\hertz}$), which numerical differentiation would otherwise amplify, positions in both datasets were low-pass filtered with a fourth-order Butterworth filter (\qty{200}{\hertz} cutoff). Two-dimensional velocity was estimated by central differences of the filtered positions and, for the eye-tracking data, averaged across both eyes. Power spectral densities were computed with Welch's method using a Hann window of \qty{256}{\milli\second} (256 samples) and an overlap of \qty{64}{\milli\second} (64 samples). The resulting spectra are shown in \cref{fig:fixation_psd}, with ensemble sizes and confidence ribbons.

\subsubsection*{Importance and discovery of gamma-scale fixational dynamics}
Velocity power spectra of fixational periods show a broadband peak in both the modelled and human data (\cref{fig:fixation_psd}\textbf{a,b)}). The modelled trajectories peak at \qty{78.12}{\hertz} with a narrow peak and near-identical X- and Y-velocity spectra (\cref{fig:fixation_psd}a). The human data peak at \qty{58.59}{\hertz} with a markedly broader peak. The peak power of the human data is roughly an order of magnitude below that of the model. We can also extract velocity autocorrelation functions for the data and model, shown in \cref{fig:fixation_psd}\textbf{c,d)}. This gives an independent data point which estimates the typical fixational reversal time, which we model through a harmonic oscillation. The first minimum in the autocorrelation, $\tau_{\min}$, is then interpreted as a half period ($f = 1/2\tau_{\min}$). The modelled velocity autocorrelation shows damped oscillations over several cycles, with $\tau_{\min}=\qty{6.112}{\milli\second}$ and then $f=\qty{81.81}{\hertz}$, in good agreement with its spectral peak, as shown in \cref{fig:fixation_psd}\textbf{c)}. The human velocity autocorrelation instead shows a single negative lobe (minimum ${\approx}\,{-0.35}$ to $-0.4$) that decays to zero rapidly, within approximately \qty{20}{\milli\second}. The human data thus give $\tau_{\min}=\qty{6.625}{\milli\second}$ and hence $f=\qty{75.47}{\hertz}$ (\cref{fig:fixation_psd}\textbf{d)}. The \qty{17}{\hertz} offset between this estimate and the human spectral peak is consistent with a broadband, weakly coherent process, in contrast to the narrowband, coherent oscillation of the model.

The human spectral peak lies well below the \qtyrange{80}{90}{\hertz} range that generally characterises mean tremor frequency in the literature, with a slightly larger amplitude. This may indicate different neural dynamics during visual exploration than during prolonged fixation. This interpretation finds a parallel in the finding that cortical gamma frequency is increased when viewing local regions of high contrast \cite{han_gamma_2022, Adjamian2008-rz, RAY2010885}, where prolonged fixation may correspond to a tightly constrained control of the eye, analogous to exploring a visual location with high contrast. The broad power spectrum shown in \cref{fig:fixation_psd}\textbf{b)} and the quickly attenuated velocity autocorrelation in \cref{fig:fixation_psd}\textbf{d)} are interpreted as the influence of changing gamma rhythms of OMT across trials and subjects, as well as the effects of other high amplitude components of fixation, drift and microsaccades, which will broaden the spectral decomposition of fixations. Addressing these factors in designing further experiments is considered in \cref{sec:spatiotemporal_predictions}
