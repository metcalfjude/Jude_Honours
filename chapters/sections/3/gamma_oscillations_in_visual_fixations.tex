\section{Gamma oscillations during visual fixation}\label{sec:gamma_oscillations}
We now look to verifying the short-time and fine spatial scales of our model predictions, specifically of those high frequencies found in local sampling of potential modes in \cref{sec:local_harmonic_oscillations}. Typically short-time gaze dynamics are divided into microsaccades, low-frequency linear drift, and high frequency ocular tremor \cite{rucci_control_2015}. There is increasing evidence that microsaccades---which is the name given to jumps in gaze during fixaton---are driven by the same statistics and process as regular (called macro-) saccades. This is predicted by our model, where saccades on all scales are manifestations of the Lévy flight jump process; a full analysis of the superdiffusion of such saccades on the micro- and macro-scales is covered in the next section (\cref{sec:search_superdiffusion}). Linear drifts in fixation have been found to be important in gathering information. An account of low-frequency linear drift easily fits into the model presented here, for instance as steep peak in the dynamical kernel or a simple drift in the potential landscape, producing a self-avoiding walk as described in \cite{engbert_integrated_2011} and described in \cref{tab:search_models_colored}. But our focus here is that of the high frequency signal called microtremor often found in eye movements and misattributed to noise \cite{rucci_control_2015, rucci_miniature_2007, rucci_temporal_2018, rucci_unsteady_2015, bowers_effects_2019, roberts_fixational_2013, wu_fixational_2024, greschner_retinal_2002, graham_ocular_2023}, but which we predict to be important to the function of a visual search.

The current study is the first to make a well-motivated prediction concerning high-frequency ocular microtremor as a gamma-scale sampling pattern linked to deeper neural activity. Identifying ocular tremor as high frequency gamma-scale visual sampling is a novel and useful approach in understanding and quantifying perceptual sampling. This is supported by recent evidence that ocular microtremor may have a shared origin for both eyes in the brainstem \cite{graham_ocular_2023}, and coarse results that neural disorders such as Parkinson's' disease effect tremor frequency across all tasks \cite{kaski_ocular_2017}.
\subsection{Previous treatment as ocular microtremor}

Ocular microtremor is the smallest-amplitude component of fixational eye motion. It has been observed across experimental studies, with reported amplitudes of up to \qtyrange{30}{40}{\sec} of arc \cite{ryle_simultaneous_2015, alkalbani2010thesis}. Despite its small amplitude, microtremor has been proposed to contribute to the dynamic sampling of high-contrast features and fine spatial detail \cite{rucci_miniature_2007, rucci_temporal_2018, rucci_unsteady_2015, roberts_fixational_2013}. However, a recent physical study concluded that its magnitude may be insufficient to produce a meaningful change in the retinal image during prolonged fixation \cite{bowers_effects_2019}. We therefore consider the hypothesis that the role of microtremor will be task-dependant: it may have a more important functional role during visual search, when gaze is rapidly adjusted in response to incoming information, than during prolonged, stationary fixation. Further, the role of microtremor has been demonstrated to be important in accounting for further brain function, such as being a biomarker for neurological fatigue \cite{lyapunov2022fatigue}, brain state during anaesthesia (under which the tremor frequency will halve) \cite{bojanic2001anaesthesia, heaney2004anesthesia, kevin2002bis}, and as a biomarker for schizophrenia \cite{shoshina2023procedia}.

In our search-task data, the tremor spectrum has a broad peak at a lower frequency than is typically reported, where we find a peak at \qty{56}{\hertz}, and with a larger estimated amplitude. By comparison, studies of longer fixation periods generally report more localised peaks around \qtyrange{80}{90}{\hertz} \cite{graham_ocular_2023}.

The present analysis differs from the existing literature in two important respects:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We examine eye movements during a naturalistic visual search task. The major studies in this field, as for instance surveyed in the influential review by \textcite{graham_ocular_2023}, instead measure tremor across a prolonged fixation, typically lasting more than \qty{3000}{\milli \second} \cite{ko_eye_2016, alkalbani2010thesis, ryle_simultaneous_2015}.
    \item The data were not collected specifically to measure this high-frequency component. Ocular microtremor is commonly studied using velocity or acceleration signals, which emphasize high-frequency motion. In our data, the estimated tremor amplitude is larger than values often reported: approximately \qty{200}{\sec} of arc, compared with values of up to \qty{40}{\sec} of arc in prior studies \cite{ryle_simultaneous_2015, alkalbani2010thesis}. Because the recordings and analyses were not optimised for direct comparison of tremor amplitude, this difference should be interpreted cautiously.
\end{enumerate}
The fact that the following analysis can be done on the positional data motivates a closer study on the fixational tremor frequency during the rapid fixations which occur during visual search. In particular, testing the hypothesis that this high amplitude gamma-scale microtremor is a sampling process can be tested rigorously through experimental designs presented in \cref{sec:spatiotemporal_predictions}.

\subsection{Analysis of fixational Fourier spectra}

We analyse the spectral properties of fixational gaze trajectories during visual search. The shorter, more naturalistic fixations in our data pose a challenge for estimating high-frequency motion. In our data collected from \textcite{naher_primate_saccades} fixations contain on average approximately \qty{200}{\milli\second} of data, which yields only about ten cycles of a \qty{50}{\hertz} signal. Moreover, estimating high-frequency motion from noisy position measurements can be dangerous, as the white noise often present in these detectors often approaches tremor amplitude \cite{ko_eye_2016, ryle_simultaneous_2015}.

Our recordings were sampled at \qty{500}{\hertz}, with reported positional precision of up to \qty{36}{\sec} of arc \cite{srresearcheyelink1000specs}. Because low-frequency drift can dominate the positional power spectrum, we analyse velocity rather than position. The expectation of the position spectrum is that it will scale approximately as $\mathrm{PSD}_{\mathbf{x}}(f) \sim f^{-2}$, which is the assumption for tremor-less gaze fixations modelled with Brownian noise \cite{engbert_normal, engbert_integrated_2011}. Velocity, as found through differentiation will multiply spectral power by a factor by  $(2 \pi f)^2$, yielding an approximately flat velocity spectrum with no peak. Thus, under a baseline model with no distinct tremor component, we would expect no peak in the velocity spectrum, instead just a flat baseline with $\mathrm{PSD}_{\mathbf{v}}(f) \sim f^{0}$.

The analysis pipeline is as follows. We apply a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff of \qty{200}{\hertz} to reduce high-frequency instrumental noise. We then estimate each eye's two-dimensional velocity by central differencing the filtered position data and compute the Fourier spectrum of the resulting velocity signals. The sampling rate gives a Nyquist frequency of \qty{250}{\hertz}; the chosen filter cutoff is below this limit.
