
\section{Behavioural data}\label{sec:behavioural_data}
To ground the physical background of this thesis, this section reviews the empirical behavioural data which characterise human visual inference. The visual system does not operate as a passive receiver of sensory input, but rather as an active, dynamic sampler of a changing environment \cite{friston_does_2018}. This active sensing manifests across two coupled physical observables: the internal dynamics of rich neural oscillatory networks, and the external, mechanical execution of gaze trajectories. Crucially, these domains inform and drive each other, and each exhibit complex, scale-free dynamics in their activity. Characterised by background spectra with power law tails and anomalous diffusion, the visual system is capable of devoting attention to many spatiotemporal scales of event. Viewing these biological phenomena through the lens of statistical mechanics provides a rigorous foundation for mathematically modelling the visual system. This section establishes these empirical constraints by first examining the functional role of neural frequency bands and theta-gamma coupling (\cref{subsec:neur_freq_bands}), before detailing the multi-scale, stochastic regimes of human gaze trajectories (\cref{subsec:gaze_traj}). Together, these behavioural patterns motivate the fractional dynamics given by the FNS equations of motion, and the developments upon that model in the subsequent sections.

\subsection{Timescales of neural computation}\label{subsec:neur_freq_bands}
Throughout much of this thesis, links are drawn between timescales of interactions and dynamics in the FNS model and well-studied frequency bands of activity in the human brain. It is then valuable to outline the studied computational and functional interpretations of such frequency bands. Neural firing activity is divided into frequency bands named by greek letters, originally discovered and decomposed from electroencephalogram (EEG) data. Divisions are only approximate, but for this thesis these bands are: delta (\SIrange{0.1}{4}{\hertz}), theta (\SIrange{4}{8}{\hertz}), alpha (\SIrange{8}{14}{\hertz}), beta (\SIrange{14}{30}{\hertz}), and gamma (\SIrange{30}{220}{\hertz}) \cite{fan_relation_2007, klimesch_frequency_2018}. Of particular interest to this thesis are the interpretations of theta and gamma neural activity as functionally important to attention and search tasks of computation. Theta rhythms have been shown to modulate attention during auditory tasks \cite{wang_attention_2022, yang_neural_2024}, visual search tasks \cite{kawashima_attentional_2022, spyropoulos_theta_2018, Mo_competing_2019}, and in analysing the general attentional mechanism of the brain \cite{tan_theta_2024, fan_relation_2007, klimesch_frequency_2018}. Intervals between theta peaks are finding stronger interpretation in the neuroscience literature as discretely sampling the environment for integration into the internal model of the brain. Strong direct evidence for this theory include correlations between intelligence and high theta peak power in EEG data during tasks requiring focus and attention \cite{wang_attention_2022, tan_theta_2024}, and the discovered sensitivity of the visual system to react and search scenes on a theta rhythm \cite{kawashima_attentional_2022, spyropoulos_theta_2018}. Accordingly, the firing patterns which define the lowest level of information carried in the brain are regularly found to be gamma in scale, almost always within \SIrange{30}{100}{\hertz} \cite{canolty_high_2006, harris2026-cn, ursino_thetagamma_2024, brooks_thetagamma_2020, tort_thetagamma_2009}. The coupling between the phase of theta activity and the amplitude of gamma activity is known as theta-gamma coupling (TGC). This phenomenon, a specific form of phase-amplitude coupling (PAC), is schematically represented in \cref{fig:litrev-thetagamma_coupling}. The evidence and theory behind TGC are described in a systematic review by \textcite{ursino_thetagamma_2024}. This thesis provides a computation view of the origin and function of TGC in the visual system as modulating periods of attention during which fixations exhibit dynamics of active sensing. 
\subsection{Gaze trajectories}\label{subsec:gaze_traj}
Healthy human gaze trajectories consist of different dynamical regimes. The most studied pattern of gaze trajectories are their division into periods of fixation, interspersed by near-instantaneous jumps between fixations, these jumps being known as \textit{saccades} \cite{erkelens_coordination_2006, seung_how_1996, lencastre_dynamical_2025, benitezy_eyetracking_1970}. Importantly, this behaviour of local movements interspersed with large discontinuous jumps suggest modelling eye movements through a Lévy flight. This paradigm has a limited history in the literature, and fitting data to a simple phenomenological Lévy flight parameterised by the stable parameter $\alpha$ has found some limited use as a biomarker for ADHD \cite{papanikolaou_levy_2024}. Broader saccade metrics have been found to be valuable as non-invasive biomarkers for broader neural disorders, including schizophrenia \cite{caldani_saccadic_2017, morita_eye_2017, zhu_diagnosis_2024, lindner_disorders_2005, zhu_diagnosis_2024}, autism \cite{bittencourt_saccadic_2013, wang_saccadic_2025}, OCD \cite{bittencourt_saccadic_2013}, bipolar disorder \cite{wang_saccadic_2025, bittencourt_saccadic_2013}, and Parkinson's disease \cite{wang_saccadic_2025, bittencourt_saccadic_2013}. The dynamics of fixation have received less scholarly attention as a biomarker of cognition. Gaze fixations exhibit three distinct dynamical patterns: near-instant microsaccades within the same point of focus, slow ocular drift about a point of focus, and high frequency ocular micro tremor \cite{van_ede_looking_2026,graham_ocular_2023, engbert_integrated_2011, spauschus_origin_1999}. Detailed examinations and systematic reviews of these behaviours have not considered their computational role in active sensing and efficient search. The work presented in this research strongly motivate a closer and more biologically informed understanding of the dynamics of gaze trajectories, across a number of spatial and temporal scales, to diagnose and understand the function of global functioning of the brain, much of which controls the visual system.
