\section{Probabilistic computation}\label{sec:comp_sampling}
Computation in the brain must account for high levels of uncertainty. Sensory inputs are noisy, high-dimensional, and ambiguous, so inference in the brain must be represented by some distribution of activity and decisions, rather than a single deterministic decision. The stochastic dynamical exploration of an information landscape, known as probabilistic computation, offers a practical framework to understand the variability of a sampling process, to explore an internal model of the world and inform decision-making. Considering neural activity, the explored landscape represents a target distribution of neural firing, and a sampling process approximates this distribution through stochastic samples (see \cref{fig:litrev-thetagamma_coupling}). From an algorithmic perspective, an effective sampler should produce near-independent samples which decorrelate quickly and explore robustly and dynamically \cite{delacour_neural_1997, dong_neural_nodate}.

This paradigm of a sampling process as probabilistic computation underlies modern machine learning and statistics, where generative models learn patterns from sparse samples of large multivariate distributions \cite{delacour_neural_1997, cranmer_advances_2023}. It has also gained traction in for biological computation, in which the variability of neural population activity is not a nuisance but a functional signature of an internal model of perceptual uncertainty \cite{terada_chaotic_2024, koch_predicting_1999, lange_bayesian_2020}. This section outlines the neural sampling hypothesis as an interpretation of variability in neuronal firing patterns (\cref{subsec:neur_sampling}), introduces fractional neural sampling (FNS) as a model of the spatiotemporal structure of that variability, and then extends the framework to visual search as a sampling process (\cref{subsec:visual_sampling}).

\subsection{Neural sampling}\label{subsec:neur_sampling}
The neural sampling hypothesis proposes that the variability of cortical responses is a functional feature of computation \cite{orban_neural_2016}. Stochastic activity patterns, otherwise regarded as noise, are thus considered to be samples drawn from a posterior distribution over possible sensory interpretations \cite{orban_neural_2016, NIPS2002_a486cd07}. Uncertainty is then encoded in the spread of these responses across time and trials, with wider response distributions reflecting greater uncertainty \cite{NIPS2002_a486cd07, delacour_neural_1997, orban_neural_2016}. The hypothesis has accounted for network-level patterns of activity in V1 better than alternative theories \cite{NIPS2002_a486cd07, ujfalussy_sampling_2022, orban_neural_2016}, and energy-based models offer a biologically plausible implementation \cite{dong_neural_nodate}.

Variability need not arise from true stochastic noise. Spatially extended, balanced excitatory--inhibitory (E--I) circuits can generate rich variability intrinsically, producing localised activity patterns that wander and switch between locations \cite{chen_spatiotemporal_2022}. Gaussian and Poisson noise models drive diffusive exploration which is normally diffusive with no dominant timescale. In these models, sampling mixes slowly and are highly correlated, tending to remain trapped in a single mode of a multimodal distribution \cite{neal_sampling_1996, latuszynski_mcmc_2025}. The dynamics of E--I networks appear to be richer than this, which has motivated functional models that treat the structure of neural variability as part of the computation itself.

\subsubsection*{Fractional neural sampling (FNS)}
FNS is one such model \cite{qi_fractional_2022}. It describes the centre of mass of a localised spiking pattern as a Lévy flight (see \cref{subsec:levy_motion}) whose long-time superdiffusive statistics match an uncertain and complex multimodal target probability distribution more quickly than similar models based on Brownian motion. The model is called fractional because the non-local dynamics of this superdiffusive motion require fractional calculus to describe \cite{tarasov_general_2021} (see \cref{sec:levy_superdiffusion} for such a treatment). FNS dynamics capture the rich spatiotemporal dynamics of cortical activity patterns of travelling waves through the E--I circuit models of neural activity, which capture the neural coupling between theta-rhythmic organisation of gamma-scale sampling \cite{chen_spatiotemporal_2022}, as shown in \cref{fig:litrev-thetagamma_coupling}. FNS therefore links the rich temporal and spatially superdiffusive dynamics of neural firing patterns to the powerful neural sampling framework. 

The principal advantage of FNS dynamics over standard continuous random walks in describing neural sampling in the brain is its ability to efficiently sample complex, dynamical, and multimodal distributions with far-apart modes, a feature of natural environments replete with multiple distinct salient objects. While Brownian samplers are trapped by high potential energy barriers, the heavy-tailed jumps of FNS allow the sampler to switch freely between modes, balancing exploitation of known high-probability regions with exploration of uncertain ones \cite{simsekli_fractional_nodate, metzler_random_2000, chen_spatiotemporal_2022}. Furthermore, because the movement of the eyes is driven by E--I neural circuits that are expected to preserve key features of the model, the FNS description can be applied to gaze dynamics.
\subsection{Visual sampling}\label{subsec:visual_sampling}
This thesis presents visual search tasks as such a paradigm where a sampling model is well motivated, and driven by the underlying neural sampling process as well. The visual system is evolved to actively and efficiently process noisy information and evidence from the external world, and plan future search paths to gather further noisy evidence \cite{Renninger2005-ee, renninger_where_2007, najemnik_optimal_2005, bujia_modeling_2022}. The brain is hypothesised to create a map of the most interesting areas of a visual landscape, to decide where to look first, called a saliency map \cite{ZHAOPING2026103211}. Here, saliency is equivalent to the Boltzmann log-likelihood potential presented in \cref{eq:boltzmann-gibbs} from the probability distribution of fixations $p(\textbf{x})$, $S(\textbf{x}) = -\log(p(\textbf{x}))$, where such a saliency map informs the exploration of gaze as it samples highly salient areas rapidly \cite{ZHAOPING2026103211, Bruce_2009_Saliency}. As such, undirected visual searching of an image will show the areas most salient to the brain, allowing insight into the computational sampling mechanism of the visual system. In particular, search tasks require sophisticated integration of the cost and expected information gain of decisions, which invites fruitful decision analysis \cite{Wedel_2023}. Empirical experiments show that eye movements show good agreement with greedy local information maximising fixations, given well-motivated visual sensitivity based on human physiology \cite{Radulescu_2026, najemnik_optimal_2005, renninger_where_2007}. Any development of a physics-inspired and biologically-motivated model of visual search must then balance informational efficiency with accurate physical description.

%\subsection{Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods}\label{subsec:MCMC}
%This view of neural- and visual sampling of a target distribution motivates analogy to a foundational set of methods in machine learning. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods have become the algorithmic basis for generating samples from target distributions in rigorous Bayesian inference in machine learning \cite{cranmer_advances_2023, latuszynski_mcmc_2025, speagle_conceptual_2020, andrieu_particle_2010}. MCMC operates by constructing a Markov chain, where the transition matrix is calculated such that the equilibrium distribution of samples is the desired target distribution. In the context of neural computation, biological circuits are often modelled as implementing continuous-time variants of MCMC sampling of neural population targets, most notably through Langevin sampling and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) \cite{latuszynski_mcmc_2025, betancourt_conceptual_2018}. Langevin sampling dynamics utilise local gradient information from the probability landscape, combined with Gaussian white noise, to explore the state space over time. Such dynamics are identical to the Langevin system studied in statistical physics, described in \cref{eq:langevin}, where $V(\textbf{x}) = - \log(\pi(\textbf{x}))$ is the potential function over parameters $\textbf{x}$ required yielding a distribution $\pi(\textbf{x})$ of samples. Despite their ubiquity, traditional MCMC methods encounter severe theoretical and practical limitations when exploring unknown, heavy-tailed, and/or multimodal target distributions. Standard Langevin and HMC dynamics are driven by continuous Brownian and Hamiltonian motion respectively, and the traversal of low-probability regions, equivalent to dynamical energy barriers, becomes exponentially slow. Consequently, the mean exit time for the sampler to leave one mode and discover another grows exponentially with the modal separation distance \cite{latuszynski_mcmc_2025}. To accelerate this sluggish exploration, algorithms often introduce auxiliary momentum variables to drive global landscape traversal, as in the FNS equations of motion \ref{eq:fns}. However, even with momentum-based acceleration, traversing deep energy valleys remains highly inefficient \cite{latuszynski_mcmc_2025, neal_sampling_1996, betancourt_conceptual_2018}. The nonlocal dynamics introduced in fractional sampling are one branch of research into how rapid mixing of samples in complex distributions can be achieved without the need for nontrivial parameter tuning and knowledge of the distribution of interest. For instance, fractional algorithms based on Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling and second-order Langevin sampling with a momentum variable have been briefly developed and explored \cite{simsekli_fractional_nodate, ijcai2018p419, qi_fractional_2022, oechsler_levy_2024, behme_levy_2026}. Importantly, FNS dynamics over a probability landscape yield a mean exit time between modes which scales linearly rather than exponentially with separation \cite{qi_fractional_2022}. FNS dynamics thus demonstrate a profound computational advantage for efficient biological inference through it's background as an effective sampling algorithm.
