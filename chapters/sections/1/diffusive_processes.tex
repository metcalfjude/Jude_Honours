\section{Diffusive processes}\label{sec:diffusive_processes}
In formalising empirical observations of human gaze and neural activity into a mathematical model, this thesis relies on the framework of non-equilibrium statistical mechanics and stochastic calculus. When a system is driven by a massive number of microscopic interactions—such as the complex interconnected firing of a neural population—modelling the exact deterministic dynamics becomes intractable. Instead, statistical physics provides the tools to coarse-grain these high-dimensional systems into low-dimensional stochastic differential equations. This section establishes the mathematical progression necessary to model scale-free visual inference. We first review the classical paradigm of normal diffusion and Brownian motion (\cref{subsec:brownian_motion}), which serves as the foundational model for standard diffusive physical systems. We then examine how systems of interest frequently violate the classical assumptions of normal diffusion and Brownian motion, thereby clarifying the presence and role of anomalous diffusion in many complex dynamical systems (\cref{subsec:anom_diff}). Finally, we introduce Lévy stable motion (\cref{subsec:levy_motion}), a class of superdiffusive fractional stochastic processes that mathematically capture the heavy-tailed, intermittent dynamics characteristic of optimal visual search and neural activity.
\subsection{Brownian motion and normal diffusion}\label{subsec:brownian_motion}
Statistical modelling has been fundamental in understanding complex dynamical systems: separating the dynamics of macroscopic observables into deterministic terms and stochastic terms reduces chaotic high-dimensional systems to a small number of variables of interest \cite{hassler_stochastic_2016, cunchala2024basicoverviewvariousstochastic}. Stochastic processes are often the most natural, tractable, and accurate modelling tools when examining high-dimensional chaotic systems across physics \cite{leroux_random_2021, Einstein1905, uhlenbeck1930theory}, machine learning \cite{cunchala2024basicoverviewvariousstochastic, surjanovic_autosgd_2025, chen_anomalous_2022}, and neuroscience \cite{orban_neural_2016, delacour_neural_1997, NIPS2002_a486cd07}. The universality of these stochastic models is a result of the central limit theorem applied to the fluctuations in these systems: the distribution of the summed effect of a large number of identical and independently distributed (i.i.d.) random variables of finite mean and variance will approach a Gaussian \cite{ulyanov2024classical, heyde1978central, gnedenko1954limit}.

The most ubiquitous stochastic process in statistical physics is the Brownian motion $W_t$, also called the Wiener process, which has stationary, independent Gaussian increments over all non-overlapping time intervals. A Brownian motion is defined only by its timescale, with a variance over trajectories after time $t$ of $t$ itself, a scaling called normal diffusion. The universality of Brownian motion can be seen in the variety of processes which it can model: from the movement of a macroscopic particle in a non-turbulent fluid \cite{hassler_stochastic_2016} to the gravitational influence of an orbiting collection of stars on supermassive black holes \cite{Chandrasekhar1943, Merritt2007a}. The generalisation of the Brownian motion for heavy-tailed i.i.d. variables with diverging variance is a Lévy flight, and is described in detail in \cref{subsec:levy_motion}. The comparison of these processes is shown in \cref{fig:brownian_vs_levy}.

\subsubsection*{Langevin dynamics of Brownian motion}
Brownian motion of a variable $\textbf{x}_t$ of arbitrary dimension through time $t$ about a potential landscape $V(\textbf{x})$ can be described in terms of the overdamped Langevin equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:langevin}
    d\textbf{x}_t = -\gamma \nabla V(\textbf{x}_t) dt + \sqrt{2 \gamma} dW_t 
\end{equation}
where the constant $\gamma$ describes noise strength \cite{smoluchowski1906zur, langevin1908theorie, uhlenbeck1930theory, kramers1940brownian} and $dW_t$ is a Brownian motion. Although the dynamics of $\textbf{x}_t$ are described stochastically, the dynamics of the corresponding probability distribution $P(\textbf{x},t)$ are deterministic and can be described exactly. Such an equation of motion of $P(\textbf{x},t)$ under any stochastic dynamics defining $\textbf{x}_t$ is called the Fokker-Planck equation \cite{boffi_probability_2023, jordan_variational_1998, wang1945theory, kolmogoroff1931analytischen, planck1917ueber, fokker1914mittlere}. In the case of \cref{eq:langevin}, the Fokker-Planck equation is \cite{jordan_variational_1998, boffi_probability_2023, planck1917ueber}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:overdamped-fokkerplanck}
    \partial_t P(\textbf{x},t) = \gamma \nabla \cdot \left( P(\textbf{x},t) \nabla V(\textbf{x}) + \nabla P(\textbf{x},t) \right)
\end{equation}
whose unique stationary distribution is the Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boltzmann-gibbs}
    \pi(\textbf{x}) \propto \exp[-V(\textbf{x})]
\end{equation}
which yields the important relationship, where a 
Langevin potential can be constructed from a normalised target density via $V(\textbf{x}) = -\ln \pi(\textbf{x})$, useful in constructing effective potentials from empirical data where only the likelihood of fixations $\pi(\textbf{x})$ is given. Importantly, the Langevin system Fokker-Planck \cref{eq:overdamped-fokkerplanck} will describe the diffusion of a sharply peaked initial distribution, and the statistics of this diffusion from a sharp initial condition define normal diffusion. The solution to the Fokker-Planck equation from an exact initial condition $P(\textbf{x},0) = \delta^n(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_0)$ is dominated over short times by the $\nabla P$ term as $\nabla P \gg P \nabla V$, reducing the Fokker-Planck equation to the heat equation $\partial_t P = \gamma\nabla^2 P$. The leading-order short-time solution is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal_diffusion}
    P(\textbf{x}, \Delta t) \propto \exp \left[ -\frac{\|\textbf{x} - \textbf{x}_0 \|^2}{4 (\gamma \Delta t)}\right] \implies \text{Var}[\textbf{x}(\Delta t)] \propto \gamma \Delta t
\end{equation}
which demonstrates the universal behaviour which defines normal diffusion: the short-time scaling of the position variance is linear in time, as $\text{Var}[\textbf{x}_{\Delta t}] = 2 \gamma \Delta t$ \cite{klafter_anomalous_2005, vilk_strong_2025, sposini_towards_2022, oliveira_anomalous_2019}. Thus, the diffusion coefficient $\gamma$ is the timescale of Brownian motion. Understanding systems whose fluctuations do not exhibit such normal diffusion is central to understanding stochastic motion and complex dynamical systems.

The diffusion arising from such small uncorrelated perturbations of a dynamical system leads to the statistical properties which first drove the study of statistical physics, such as normal diffusion and the universality of Brownian motion, which we follow in \cref{subsec:anom_diff} and \cref{subsec:levy_motion} respectively, however the neural system described in this thesis diverges strongly from the Brownian paradigm, requiring an understanding of fractional processes.

Of interest in this thesis is the case where microscopic perturbations become correlated, yielding heavy-tailed statistics. Correlated steps, long-time memory effects, and chaotic microscopic dynamics can result in a stochastic process that is not limited to a Brownian motion \cite{Brevitt2025, Klages2013}. Of interest to us is the fractional \textit{Lévy flight}, a process driven by scale-free noise that spans across many orders of magnitude. For instance, a spiking neural network with diverging activity correlation length, where the centre of mass of neural firing is then subject to large jump discontinuities \cite{La_Camera_2022_meanfieldneuron, qi_fractional_2022, qi_fractional_2022}. Such large jumps in time series are modelled with Lévy stable noise, where the increments are drawn independently from a heavy-tailed distribution. This section describes the analytics, stability, and behaviour of such Lévy processes in our current study, and how their diffusive properties differ from those of Brownian motion.

\subsection{Anomalous diffusion}\label{subsec:anom_diff}
The universality of normal diffusion in systems driven by Brownian motion, as above, has been studied closely, most notably in the movement of particles in a fluid \cite{Einstein1905, klafter_anomalous_2005, metzler_anomalous_2014, Merritt2007a}. These dynamics inform the definition of normal diffusion through this variance scaling with time, rigorously calculated as the mean squared displacement (MSD) $\langle \|\Delta \textbf{x} (\Delta t) \|^2 \rangle \propto 
\Delta t$. However, increasingly interesting physical systems have been found which diffuse nonlinearly in time, a situation known as anomalous diffusion, with an MSD scaling as:
\begin{equation}
    \langle \| \Delta \textbf{x} (\Delta t) \|^{2} \rangle \propto \Delta t^{\lambda_2}, \ \ \lambda_2 \neq 1
\end{equation}
Such anomalous diffusion yields two cases: subdiffusion, $0 < \lambda_2 < 1$, where diffusion scales more slowly than normal, and superdiffusion, $1 < \lambda_2 < 2$, where diffusion scales faster than normal. The case of $\lambda_2 = 2$ is known as ballistic diffusion, as it is found in the deterministic case of constant velocity $x = vt$. 

Systems displaying anomalous diffusion are self-similar and exhibit scale-free fractal spatiotemporal fluctuations due to continuous-time power-law diffusive scaling. It is advantageous in the study of anomalous superdiffusion to capture this diffusive self-similarity across scales through an extension of the $\lambda_2$--scaling of MSD to a spectrum of absolute moments $\lambda(q)$. This spectrum describes the diffusive self-similarity from the small spatial scales (low $q$) to large spatial scales (high $q$). Such a spectrum of absolute moments $\lambda(q)$ to capture diffusive scaling is \cite{vilk_strong_2025}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MFD}
	\langle \| \Delta \textbf{x}(\Delta t) \| ^ q \rangle \propto \Delta t^{\lambda(q)}, \ \ q > 0
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the order of the fractional absolute moment, with $q=2$ recovering the MSD, and $q=1$ yielding a mean absolute displacement (MAD), and so on for any value $q>0$. Systems exhibiting so-called strong anomalous diffusion (SAND) or multifractality will have a nonlinear spectrum $\lambda(q)$, which is interpreted as different statistical scaling properties on different scales (for instance, a Brownian particle which exhibits rare large jumps \cite{wang_strong_2020}). A process which is uniformly self-similar on all scales will be defined by a linear spectrum $\lambda(q) = \frac{q}{\alpha}$. We will see that both our gaze-dynamics data and the model we develop are monoscaling across many scales of $q$ (see \cref{subsec:levy_motion} and \cref{sec:levy_superdiffusion}).

\subsubsection*{Anomalous superdiffusion}\label{subsubsec:anomalous_superdiffusion}
Of importance in studying the dynamics of visual search, the pattern of superdiffusive motion is found in many complex biological systems. We model the efficient exploration of gaze dynamics using Lévy motion, the standard dynamical model of superdiffusion in physics, which is presented in detail in \cref{subsec:levy_motion}. Such Lévy superdiffusion is closely linked to the problem of designing a strategic search for scarce resources. The optimality of a superdiffusive process in a search has been reproduced in diverse functional search models \cite{humphries_optimal_2014, campeau_evolutionary_2022, sims_optimal_2019}, in a population of diverse searching agents with a range of superdiffusive exponents $\lambda_2$ \cite{clementi_search_2021} as well as in a large study against several different search conditions \cite{humphries_optimal_2014}, and have been reported in the empirical search paths of biological organisms from the flights of drosophila flies \cite{sims_optimal_2019, reynolds_free-flight_2007}, albatross' searches \cite{focardi_levy_2014, humphries_new_2013}, and strongly across many marine predators \cite{sims_scaling_2008, humphries_environmental_2010}. This thesis provides a biologically realistic and well-motivated superdiffusive search for research into a case for optimal Lévy motion. See \textcite{reynolds_liberating_2015} for an in-depth discussion of a richer view of biological Lévy superdiffusion.
\subsection{Lévy motion}\label{subsec:levy_motion}
\begin{figure}[h!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/BvL.pdf}
	\caption{\textbf{Schematic representations of a normally diffusive Brownian motion and a Superdiffusive Lévy flight.} \textbf{a)} Schematic representation of a 2D Brownian motion, along with its time-averaged mean absolute displacement (TAMAD), which is the $q=1$ mean fractional displacement as in \cref{eq:MFD}. Normal diffusion is present here as a power-law slope with exponent $1/2$, giving a diffusive exponent $\ lambda_1 = 1/2$ and $\alpha = 1/\lambda_1 = 2.0$. \textbf{b)} Schematic of a superdiffusive 2D Lévy flight with characteristic exponent of $\alpha=1.2$, and a MAD slope of $\lambda_1 = 5/6 = 0.833\ldots$ corresponding to the theoretical superdiffusive coefficient $\alpha = 1/\lambda_1 = 1.2$.}
	\label{fig:brownian_vs_levy}
\end{figure}
To mathematically capture the intermittent, scale-free nature of visual search, we turn to the Lévy flight: a superdiffusive stochastic process that lends itself to close analysis owing to its well-defined mathematical formulation. Here, the Lévy flight $L_t^{\alpha}$ is a stochastic process defined through its increments being drawn from a heavy-tailed symmetric $\alpha$-stable distribution (a $\mathcal{S}\alpha\mathcal{S}$ distribution), where $\alpha$ is called the stable exponent and defines the strength of superdiffusion produced. $\mathcal{S}\alpha\mathcal{S}$ distributions describe the limit of summation of i.i.d. infinite-variance variables, as a result of the generalised central limit theorem, making them ideal to describe a universality class of superdiffusive processes. The $\alpha$ value defining the $\mathcal{S}\alpha\mathcal{S}$ distribution quantifies the heaviness of its asymptotic tails, with low values of $\alpha \rightarrow 1$ producing stronger superdiffusion, and larger values $\alpha \rightarrow 2$ eventually reproducing the normally diffusive Brownian motion. The exact form of the $\mathcal{S}\alpha\mathcal{S}$ distribution of jumps $\Delta \mathbf{x}$ is defined by the characteristic function $\mathbb{E}[e^{i \Delta \textbf{x}}] = \exp[-(\gamma t)^{\alpha} {\Delta \textbf{x}}^\alpha]$, which will yield asymptotic power law tails with $P(\Delta \mathbf{x}) \sim \|\Delta \mathbf{x}\|^{-(1 + \alpha)}$. As a result, the Lévy flight $L_t^{\alpha}$ after time $t$ is defined by its fractional scaling properties and $\alpha$--stable statistics
\begin{equation} \label{eq:levy_noise}
	L_t \stackrel{d}{=} t^{1/\alpha} L_1, \ \ L_1 \sim \mathcal{S}\alpha\mathcal{S}
\end{equation}
where $\stackrel{d}{=}$ denotes equal in distribution. In the limit $\alpha \rightarrow 2$, the Lévy flight reduces to a scaled Brownian Motion $L_t^{\alpha \rightarrow 2} = \sqrt{2}W_t$. If $1 < \alpha < 2$, the variance of $L_t^{\alpha}$ diverges and has mean 0, whereas if $0 < \alpha \leq 1$, then both the variance and mean diverge. In analysing the distribution of a Lévy flight, one must generalise the standard Langevin Fokker-Planck equation (\ref{eq:overdamped-fokkerplanck}) to a Lévy motion using fractional calculus to capture nonlocal transport. To analyse this nonlocal behaviour of superdiffusion, the fractional Fokker-Planck equation is used, which yields the nonlocality and scale-free behaviour of trajectories of Lévy flights (see the appendices to \cite{qi_fractional_2022}). For an analysis of the fractional Fokker-Planck equation in this thesis, see \cref{sec:levy_superdiffusion}. Many of the simple analytic properties of the Brownian motion are no longer true of a Lévy flight. Notably, since these flights have infinite variance in step sizes, the mean squared displacement diverges. Thus, their superdiffusion is better measured using the extension of MSD to the spectrum of fractional moments defined in \cref{eq:MFD}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:levy-MFD}
	\langle \| \Delta \textbf{x}( \Delta t) \| ^ q \rangle \propto \Delta t^{\lambda(q)}, \ \ \lambda(q) = \frac{q}{\alpha} \ \ \alpha > q > 0
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the order of the fractional absolute displacement. Thus, this predicts that an $\alpha$--Lévy flight is monoscaling with spectrum $\lambda(q) = \frac{q}{\alpha}$ for $q < \alpha$. As such, for Lévy flights with exponents $\alpha > 1$ superdiffusion is well-characterised by the mean absolute displacement, where $q=1$ in \cref{eq:MFD}, and shown in \cref{fig:brownian_vs_levy}. We will see that, in a bounded potential, with momentum dynamics and lightly smoothed paths, the Lévy dynamics yield monoscaling superdiffusion for all scales $q$ beyond $ q=\alpha$. Importantly, in understanding this monoscaling, the Lévy-Itô decomposition theorem describes how a Lévy flight produces superdiffusion through its characteristic large jumps. The Lévy-Itô decomposition theorem states that the Lévy flight can be expressed as the sum of two processes: \cite{Kyprianou2014}:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item A compound Poisson process of large jumps with $\| \Delta \textbf{x} \| > 1$. Such jumps are exponentially distributed in time, and there are almost surely a finite number in any finite time period.
	\item A square-integrable jump process with mean 0 with  $\| \Delta \textbf{x} \| < 1$. This process is then finite-variance and will approach a Brownian motion when isolated from the full continuum of jumps.
\end{enumerate}
This division is useful in analysing the process in the context of nonlocal stochastic exploration in Lévy flights, which is modelled by the compound Poisson process of large jumps $\|\Delta \mathbf{x}\| > 1$, and the local exploitation dynamics modelled by the square-integrable martingale of small jumps $\|\Delta \textbf{x}\| < 1$. In the context of visual searching, such a division naturally inspires the division between large jumps of attention and periods of sustained attention. As such, we are interested in why such superdiffusive dynamics may have a powerful computational advantage in exploring an abstract, unknown landscape, especially in the context of visual search. Such computational advantages of superdiffusion, along with nested oscillations, in an abstract search task are considered in \cref{chap:functional_advantages}.
