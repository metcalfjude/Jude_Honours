\section{Functional roles of dynamical sampling patterns}\label{sec:functional_roles_of_dynamical_patterns}
The dynamics studied in this thesis are characterised by three spatiotemporal signatures: theta-scale rhythmicity, gamma-scale local oscillations, and superdiffusive spatial trajectories. The framework of \cref{chap:a_unifying_model} constructs a biologically motivated model in which these signatures emerge from the underlying physical dynamics, and \cref{chap:empirical_validations} found the same signatures in real human gaze trajectories, especially during realistic search tasks. Here, we treat the model as a sampling theory and ask what each signature contributes to an abstract sampling process within neural computation. Each signature corresponds to a model parameter: the momentum coupling $\beta$ generates local oscillations, the tail exponent $\alpha$ controls superdiffusion, and the timing of information updates sets the rhythmic organisation of sampling. We define information-theoretic metrics of search efficiency and describe how these signatures affect search, focusing on global $\alpha$--superdiffusion and local $\beta$--oscillations.
\subsection*{Information gain}\label{subsec:information_gain}
\begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/schematics/information_comparison_plot.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Schematic of dimensionless information gain.} Time and $\mathrm{IG}$ are normalised to $[0,1]$. The shaded area $A$ gives $\mathrm{SE} = 1/A - 1$; the dashed line is $\mathrm{SE}=1$. In the case of a realistic saccadic search, a typical trial will progress in steps, suddenly gaining much information, plateauing, and repeating. This inspires the use of an area metric instead of curve fitting.}
  \label{fig:ig_schematic}
\end{wrapfigure}
The central quantity in the efficiency of a strategic search is the information gain, $\mathrm{IG}(t)$. To quantify the information gathered across an abstract search belief distribution, we draw on methods from Bayesian inference and sampling. In particular, the link between the explored dynamic potential and a target-belief distribution (\cref{eq:boltzmann-gibbs}) provides a natural definition of information as belief of target location during a search. To consider both the contributions from the cross-entropy of the belief distributions $H(\pi_t,\pi_0)$, as well as the certainty in the entropy of the posterior belief ($H(\pi_t)$), we find use in the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_t \parallel \pi_0)$, which is used elsewhere in machine learning to define information gain
\begin{align}
	\mathrm{IG}(t) &= H(\pi_t, \pi_0) - H(\pi_t) \nonumber \\
	&= -\int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \pi_0(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} + \int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} \nonumber \\
	&= \int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \left( \frac{\pi_t(\mathbf{x})}{\pi_0(\mathbf{x})} \right) d\mathbf{x} \nonumber \\
	&\equiv D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_t \parallel \pi_0). \label{eq:information_gain}
\end{align}

The KL divergence is non-negative and asymmetric. In an abstract sampling model, non-negativity is interpreted in that any update away from $\pi_0$ will accumulate some amount of evidence, though not necessarily as an efficient search. A weak kernel, a slow information timescale $\tau$, or deposition in low-likelihood areas alters the belief distribution and landscape $\pi_t(\mathbf{x})$ only marginally and so produces slow growth in $\mathrm{IG}$. The curve $\mathrm{IG}(t)$ therefore summarises the global information acquired as the observer's belief landscape evolves from $\pi_0$ to $\pi_t$. The metrics in the following subsections capture different aspects of it, with typical non-dimensionalised $\mathrm{IG}(t)$ curves exhibiting different power-law behaviour, as shown in \cref{fig:ig_schematic}. The slope and growth of this information gain over time are the principal variables used to understand the efficiency of a search.

\subsection{Results}

\subsubsection*{Metrics and comparison conditions}\label{subsec:metrics}
We collected metrics from an ensemble of searches, with 250 trials per parameter set over the swept range. We considered a single sweep across $\alpha$--$\beta$ to study the joint dynamics of global superdiffusion and local oscillations. With all other parameters otherwise stated in \cref{sec:model_setup}, we sweep across $\alpha$ from $1.1$ to $2.0$ in increments of $0.05$ and $\beta$ from $0$ to $1200$ in increments of $100$.

We use three metrics that capture complementary aspects of performance: the speed of completion, the speed at which information is gathered, and the non-dimensional profile of information gain as search progresses. The first passage time ($\mathrm{FPT}$) is the time from trial onset to the first fixation on a target, perhaps the clearest measure of success, in seconds. The information velocity $\mathrm{IV}(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{IG}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}$ is the instantaneous rate of information gain, in inverse seconds. To summarise $\mathrm{IV}$ across a single trial, we time-average either the median or the mean, as stated, followed by ensemble averaging in the same way. The shape exponent $\mathrm{SE}$ describes the profile of the $\mathrm{IG}$ curve by fitting to a non-dimensionalised power law curve: we normalise time and $\mathrm{IG}$ to $[0,1]$, compute the area $A$ under the normalised curve, and define $\mathrm{SE} = 1/A - 1$. This is the exponent of the power law $\mathrm{IG} \propto t^{\mathrm{SE}}$ that has the same area as the observed monotonic curve. Thus $\mathrm{SE} = 1$ corresponds to accumulation at a constant rate, $\mathrm{SE}< 1$ to decelerating information gain, and $\mathrm{SE}>1$ to accelerating information gain. Lower $\mathrm{FPT}$ and higher $\mathrm{IV}$ and $\mathrm{SE}$ indicate better performance, the latter meaning that the rate of information gain is better sustained rather than saturating early.

To analytically reference optimal $\mathrm{FPT}$, we consider a searcher that uniformly at random and without replacement fixates at points of interest, one per theta interval. For $K$ targets among $N$ points, the expected number of fixations up to and including the first target is $(N+1)/(K+1)$\footnote{This is the expected number of failures before the first success in sampling without replacement from a hypergeometric population, $(N-K)/(K+1)$, plus the final successful fixation. See for instance \cite{Ahlgren2014-gt}.} and so, for $\tau = \qty{0.2}{\milli \second}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fpt_baseline}
  \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{FPT}_0] = t_\theta \, \frac{N+1}{K+1} = \qty{6.0919}{\second},
\end{equation}
% For N=256, K=5, t_theta=0.1422 s this evaluates to about 6.1 s -- confirm parameters.
where $t_\theta = \qty{142.22}{\milli\second}$ is the mean saccade interval for $\tau = 0.2~\unit{\milli \second}$.

\subsection*{Spatiotemporal strategies and their impact on search efficiency}

\Cref{tab:param_effects} reports the correlation between each parameter and each metric across the tested range. Decreasing values of $\mathrm{FPT}$ represent a faster average time to success, increasing $\mathrm{IV}$ represents faster information gathering, and $\mathrm{SE}$ closer to 1 represents a search which doesn't decelerate, whereas $\mathrm{SE}$ closer to 0 represents a learning curve which flattens over a trial, getting trapped exploring low probability areas. We show results for both the median and mean of these metrics to provide a full view of typical statistics and to ensure results aren't sensitive to outlier influences.

\subsubsection*{Momentum and decorrelation ($\beta$)}
Parameter $\beta$ controls the influence of short-time momentum on the dynamics of search. Because momentum will accumulate and respond to positional dynamics, it produces inertia: rather than relaxing directly into the nearest basin of the potential, the searcher retains dynamics for a short time (see the negative autocorrelation in \cref{fig:fixation_psd}\textbf{c,d)}. These same momentum-based short-time memory decorrelate future samples and drive the local dynamics of successful sampling algorithms such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo \cite{betancourt_conceptual_2018} and second-order Langevin sampling \cite{liu2025secondorderensemblelangevin}. Such decorrelation is effective in responding to local information depletion. From a biological perspective, it has been shown that such rapid fixational dynamics and decorrelation can contribute to the high resolution of the visual system, especially in high contrast or fine-grained detail in visual landscapes \cite{rucci_miniature_2007}.

The results are consistent with this interpretation (\cref{fig:beta_metrics}). Information velocity and shape exponent both correlate strongly with $\beta$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{IV}} = 0.64$ and $0.77$; $\rho_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.51$ and $0.81$ for the median and mean). Thus, momentum-memory is shown to sustain the rate of information gain. The effect on FPT is weaker: only the mean-based correlation ($\rho_{\mathrm{FPT}} = -0.48$) excludes zero, as expected if local sampling has limited influence on global exploration. Even so, the median FPT is shown to improve significantly from $\beta=0$, suggesting that the presence of $\beta$--momentum effects are most important. The benefit of $\beta$ depends on $\alpha$ (\cref{fig:joint_metrics}). Near Brownian noise (large $\alpha$), performance peaks on a narrow ridge in $\beta$ along the oscillation boundary (\cref{eq:resonant_frequency}) and falls away at other $\beta$. As $\alpha$ decreases, the ridge broadens, until at the smallest $\alpha$, performance improves monotonically with $\beta$. Superdiffusion, therefore, makes search robust to the temporal scale of $\beta$--oscillations. In summary, momentum improves information gathering, while superdiffusion largely controls global strategy.
\subsubsection*{Superdiffusion and global access ($\alpha$)}
The tail exponent $\alpha$ controls how heavy-tailed the jumps of the searcher are. At $\alpha = 2$, the noise is Gaussian, and the searcher is Brownian, so reaching a distant region requires traversing everything in between. For smaller $\alpha$, occasional long jumps give direct access to any part of the landscape. For sparse unknown targets, this strategy yields two benefits: the searcher is not confined to the region it has already sampled, and it can leave a region rapidly once remaining information has been depleted rather than diffusing slowly out of it. We therefore expect lower $\alpha$ to shorten $\mathrm{FPT}$ and to sustain information gain.

All three metrics support this claim (\cref{fig:alpha_metrics}). $\mathrm{FPT}$ increases with $\alpha$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{FPT}} = 0.55$ for the median, $0.79$ for the mean), while $\mathrm{IV}$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{IV}} = -0.75, -0.95$) and $\mathrm{SE}$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{SE}}=-0.76, -0.88$) decrease. The correlation with $\mathrm{IV}$ is the strongest in \cref{tab:param_effects}, which suggests that long jumps also raise the local rate of information gain, an interpretation supported by the literature on superdiffusive search of scarce resources \cite{gautestad_levy_2013, humphries_optimal_2014}. We therefore recover, in the presence of our additional modelled dynamics, the result that a superdiffusive trajectory best serves a sparse search.

\subsection*{Joint nested oscillations and superdiffusion}
The effects of $\beta$ and $\alpha$ reported above are not independent. \Cref{fig:joint_metrics} shows the joint dependence of the two metrics of information gathering, $\mathrm{SE}$ and $\mathrm{IV}$, on both parameters. At high $\alpha$, where search fluctuations are close to Brownian, performance is sensitively dependent on a 1-dimensional ridge in $\beta$. Both metrics are maximised along a narrow curve in the $(\alpha,\beta)$ plane, which marks where oscillations emerge in the dynamics of momentum, as defined in \cref{eq:resonant_frequency}, and performance falls away for $\beta$ larger or smaller. As $\alpha$ is lowered, this ridge broadens and is progressively lost until, at the lowest values of $\alpha$, performance improves uniformly with higher $\beta$.

A likely interpretation is that the two mechanisms contribute at different scales. Momentum improves how a region is sampled locally. Still, a sampler that relies on it alone must be tuned so that its oscillations fit the scale of the landscape, much as the trajectory length of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo must be tuned. Heavy-tailed jumps instead provide global access, whatever the local dynamics, so that a poorly chosen $\beta$ costs less when the sampler can leave an unproductive region directly. Lévy superdiffusion therefore makes the search robust to the choice of momentum regime, rather than requiring that regime to be tuned. The coupling may be computationally beneficial for this reason. Superdiffusion provides global access, momentum supplies local refinement, and together they yield a search that performs well over a wide range of parameters rather than at a single finely tuned point. This is the property sought in the minimally tuned sampler outlined in \cref{subsec:superdiffusive_probability_sampling}.

\subsection*{Summary }
Taken together, the dynamical signatures---superdiffusion and nested oscillations---studied in this chapter play complementary roles in an efficient search.
Momentum coupling $\beta$ decorrelates successive samples, raising information velocity and sustaining the rate of information gain. Superdiffusion at low $\alpha$ gives global access to the landscape, improving all metrics over the tested range, most clearly the information velocity. Rhythmic organisation organises the structure of samples, complementing spatial mechanisms with dual-scale nested oscillations.
