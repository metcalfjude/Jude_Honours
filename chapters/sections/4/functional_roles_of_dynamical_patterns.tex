\section{Functional roles of dynamical sampling patterns}\label{sec:functional_roles_of_dynamical_patterns}
The dynamics studied in this thesis are characterised by three spatiotemporal signatures: theta-scale rhythmicity, gamma-scale local oscillations, and superdiffusive spatial trajectories. The framework of \cref{chap:a_unifying_model} constructs a biologically motivated model in which these signatures emerge from the underlying physical dynamics, and \cref{chap:empirical_validations} found the same signatures in real human gaze trajectories, especially during realistic search tasks. Here we treat the model as a sampling theory and ask what each signature contributes to an abstract sampling process within neural computation. Each signature corresponds to a model parameter: the momentum coupling $\beta$ generates local oscillations, the tail exponent $\alpha$ controls superdiffusion, and the timing of information updates sets the rhythmic organisation of sampling. To quantify their roles, we define information-theoretic metrics of search efficiency and describe how each signature affects them.
\subsection*{Information gain}\label{subsec:information_gain}
\begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
  \centering

  \caption{\textbf{Schematic of dimensionless information gain.} Time and $\mathrm{IG}$ are normalised to $[0,1]$. The shaded area $A$ gives $\mathrm{SE} = 1/A - 1$; the dashed line is $\mathrm{SE}=1$.}
  \label{fig:ig_schematic}
\end{wrapfigure}
The central quantity in the efficiency of a strategic visual search is the information gain, $\mathrm{IG}(t)$. To quantify the information gathered across an abstract search landscape, we draw on Bayesian inference and sampling methods. In particular, the link between the explored dynamic potential and a target-belief distribution (\cref{eq:boltzmann-gibbs}) gives a natural definition of the information gained during a search.
% The adaptive dynamics of our model when viewed as a sampling  closely resemble well-tempered metadynamics in Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling \cite{tiwary_time-independent_2015}. Similar techniques have been used to model visual search in discrete time by \textcite{bujia_modeling_2022}, where the equivalent target distribution is the likelihood distribution for a search goal, and information is tracked only at the goal position. In \cref{sec:dynamical-information_adaptation} we expanded and unified these approaches by analysing a continuous-time dynamical-information kernel. By tracking the posterior target distribution after kernel deposition at every location in the visual scene, we model global information acquisition across an evolving landscape of belief.
To consider both the contributions from the cross-entropy of the belief distributions ($H(\pi_t,\pi_0)$), as well as the certainty in the entropy of the posterior belief ($H(\pi_t)$), we find usein the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_t \parallel \pi_0)$, which has been used elsewhere in machine learning to define information gain
\begin{align}
	\mathrm{IG}(t) &= H(\pi_t, \pi_0) - H(\pi_t) \nonumber \\
	&= -\int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \pi_0(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} + \int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} \nonumber \\
	&= \int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \left( \frac{\pi_t(\mathbf{x})}{\pi_0(\mathbf{x})} \right) d\mathbf{x} \nonumber \\
	&\equiv D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_t \parallel \pi_0). \label{eq:information_gain}
\end{align}

The KL divergence is non-negative and asymmetric. In an abstract sampling model, non-negativity means that any update away from $\pi_0$ counts as accumulated evidence, though not necessarily as efficient search. A weak kernel, a slow information timescale $\tau$, or deposition in low-likelihood areas alters the landscape only marginally and so produces slow growth in $\mathrm{IG}$. The curve $\mathrm{IG}(t)$ therefore summarises the global information acquired as the observer's belief landscape evolves from $\pi_0$ to $\pi_t$, and the metrics in the following subsections capture different aspects of it.

\subsection{Results}

\subsubsection*{Metrics and comparison conditions}\label{subsec:metrics}
We collected metrics across an ensemble of searches, with 250 trials for each parameter set across the swept range. We considered only a single sweep across the conditions otherwise stated in \cref{sec:model_setup}: an $\alpha$--$\beta$ sweep, with $\alpha$ from $1.1$ to $2.0$ in increments of $0.05$ and $\beta$ from $0$ to $1200$ in increments of $100$.

We use three metrics that capture complementary aspects of performance: the speed of completion, the speed at which information is gathered, and the temporal profile of information gain. The first passage time ($\mathrm{FPT}$) is the time from trial onset to the first fixation on a target, perhaps the clearest measure of success, in seconds. The information velocity $\mathrm{IV} = \langle\mathrm{d}\mathrm{IG}/\mathrm{d}t \rangle$ is the mean rate of information gain, in inverse seconds. The shape exponent $\mathrm{SE}$ describes the profile of the $\mathrm{IG}$ curve: we normalise time and $\mathrm{IG}$ to $[0,1]$, compute the area $A$ under the normalised curve, and define $\mathrm{SE} = 1/A - 1$. This is the exponent of the power law $\mathrm{IG} \propto t^{\mathrm{SE}}$ that has the same area as the observed monotonic curve. Thus $\mathrm{SE} = 1$ corresponds to accumulation at a constant rate, $\mathrm{SE}< 1$ to decelerating information gain, and $\mathrm{SE}>1$ to accelerating information gain. Lower $\mathrm{FPT}$ and higher $\mathrm{IV}$ and $\mathrm{SE}$ indicate better performance, the latter meaning that the rate of information gain is better sustained rather than saturating early.

As an analytical reference for optimal $\mathrm{FPT}$, we consider a searcher that fixates points uniformly at random without replacement, one per theta interval. For $K$ targets among $N$ points, the expected number of fixations up to and including the first target is $(N+1)/(K+1)$\footnote{This is the expected number of failures before the first success in sampling without replacement from a hypergeometric population, $(N-K)/(K+1)$, plus the final successful fixation. See for instance \cite{Ahlgren2014-gt}.} and so, for $\tau = \qty{0.2}{\milli \second}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fpt_baseline}
  \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{FPT}_0] = t_\theta \, \frac{N+1}{K+1} = \qty{6.0919}{\second},
\end{equation}
% For N=256, K=5, t_theta=0.1422 s this evaluates to about 6.1 s -- confirm parameters.
where $t_\theta = \qty{142.22}{\milli\second}$ is the mean saccade interval for $\tau = 0.2~\unit{\milli \second}$.

\subsection*{Spatiotemporal strategies and their impact on search efficiency}

\Cref{tab:param_effects} reports the correlation between each parameter and each metric across the tested range. Decreasing values of $\mathrm{FPT}$ represent a faster average time to success, increasing $\mathrm{IV}$ represents faster information gathering, and $\mathrm{SE}$ closer to 1 represents a search which doesn't decelerate, whereas $\mathrm{SE}$ closer to 0 represents a learning curve which flattens over a trial, getting trapped exploring low probability areas. We show results both for the median and mean of these metrics, to ensure a full view of the typical statistics, and ensure results aren't sensitive to outlier influences.

\subsubsection*{Momentum and decorrelation ($\beta$)}
The coupling $\beta$ sets how strongly the momentum variable influences the dynamics of the position of the searcher. Because momentum will tend to accumulates and respond to drift, it gives the trajectory inertia: rather than relaxing directly into the nearest basin of the potential, the searcher dynamically explores it. These same momentum-based mechanisms which decorrelate future samples drive the efficiency of successful sampling algorithms such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo \cite{betancourt_conceptual_2018} and second-order Langevin sampling \cite{liu2025secondorderensemblelangevin}, where momentum reduces the correlation between successive samples so that a fixed number of samples covers more of the distribution. In a fixation, such decorrelation is effective in responding to a depletion of information locally. From a biological perspective, it has been shown that such rapid fixational dynamics and decorrelation can produce the high resolution of the visual system, especially in high contrast or fine-grained detail in visual landscapes \cite{rucci_miniature_2007}.

The results are consistent with this interpretation (\cref{fig:beta_metrics}). The local measure, information velocity, correlates strongly with $\beta$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{IV}} = 0.64$ for the median and $0.77$ for the mean), as does the shape exponent ($\rho_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.51$ and $0.81$), indicating that gain is sustained rather than saturating early through repeated sampling of the same region. $\mathrm{FPT}$ decreases with $\beta$, but this is the weakest effect: only the mean-based correlation ($-0.48$) has a confidence interval excluding zero. This is expected from the interpretation given above of local sampling having a limited effect on global exploration. It should be said, that the existence of oscillations does seem critical however, as will be presented later in the form of a periodic boundary, which has a pronounced effect on the efficiency of dynamics. Momentum therefore has the clearest benefit for the efficiency of information gathering, and a more modest benefit for the time to reach the target. The stronger mean-based correlations suggest that its main effect is to remove slow outlier searches.

\subsubsection*{Superdiffusion and global access ($\alpha$)}
The tail exponent $\alpha$ controls how heavy-tailed the jumps of the searcher are. At $\alpha = 2$ the noise is Gaussian and the searcher is Brownian, so reaching a distant region requires traversing everything in between. For smaller $\alpha$, occasional long jumps give direct access to any part of the landscape. For sparse unknown targets this strategy yields two benefits: the searcher is not confined to the region it has already sampled, and it can leave a region rapidly once remaining information has been depleted rather than diffusing slowly out of it. We therefore expect lower $\alpha$ to shorten $\mathrm{FPT}$ and to sustain information gain.

\input{chapters/sections/4/alpha_metric}

All three metrics show this trend (\cref{fig:alpha_metrics}). $\mathrm{FPT}$ increases with $\alpha$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{FPT}} = 0.55$ for the median, $0.79$ for the mean), while $\mathrm{IV}$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{IV}} = -0.75, -0.95$) and $\mathrm{SE}$ ($\mathrm{SE}=-0.76, -0.88$) decrease. The correlation with $\mathrm{IV}$ is the strongest in \cref{tab:param_effects}, which suggests that long jumps also raise the local rate of information gain, plausibly because they explore regions more rapidly, and discover high probability modes. We therefore recover, in the presence of our additional modelled dynamics, the result that a sparse search is best served by a superdiffusive trajectory. The trend is approximately monotonic over the tested range, so this sweep does not by itself identify an interior optimum.

\subsection*{Joint nested oscillations and superdiffusion}
The effects of $\beta$ and $\alpha$ reported above are not independent. \Cref{fig:joint_metrics} shows the joint dependence of the two metrics of information gathering, $\mathrm{SE}$ and $\mathrm{IV}$, on both parameters. At high $\alpha$, where search fluctuations are close to Brownian, performance is sensitively dependant on $\beta$. Both metrics are maximised along a narrow curve in the $(\alpha,\beta)$ plane, which marks where oscillations begin in the trajectory, as defined by \cref{eq:resonant_frequency}, and fall away for $\beta$ larger or smaller. As $\alpha$ is lowered, this ridge broadens and is progressively smeared, until at the lowest values of $\alpha$ the metrics vary only weakly with $\beta$. This is a qualitative observation from a single sweep, but it is consistent across both metrics.

A likely interpretation is that the two mechanisms contribute at different scales. Momentum improves how a region is sampled locally, but a sampler that relies on it alone must be tuned so that its oscillations fit the scale of the landscape, much as the trajectory length of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo must be tuned. Heavy-tailed jumps instead provide global access whatever the local dynamics, so that a poorly chosen $\beta$ costs less when the sampler can leave an unproductive region directly. Lévy superdiffusion therefore makes the search robust to the choice of momentum regime, rather than requiring that regime to be tuned. The coupling may be computationally beneficial for this reason. Superdiffusion supplies global access, momentum supplies local refinement, and together they give a search that performs well over a wide range of parameters instead of at a single finely tuned point. This is the property sought in the minimally tuned sampler outlined in \cref{subsec:superdiffusive_probability_sampling}.

\subsection*{Summary }
Taken together, the dynamical signatures---superdiffusion and oscillations---studied in this chapter play complementary roles in an efficient search.
Momentum coupling $\beta$ decorrelates successive samples, raising information velocity and sustaining the rate of information gain. Superdiffusion at low $\alpha$ gives global access to the landscape, improving all three metrics over the tested range, most clearly the speed of information gain. Rhythmic organisation regularises the timing of samples, complementing these spatial mechanisms.
