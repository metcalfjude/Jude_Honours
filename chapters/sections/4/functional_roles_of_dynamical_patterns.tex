\section{Functional roles of dynamical patterns}\label{sec:functional_roles_of_dynamical_patterns}

The dynamics studied in this thesis are characterised by three distinct spatiotemporal signatures: theta-scale rhythmicity, gamma-scale local oscillations, and superdiffusive spatial trajectories. The framework of \cref{chap:a_unifying_model}, constructs a biologically-motivated model to produce these signatures through their emergent physical dynamics. Throughout \cref{chap:empirical_validations} we have found that the same dynamics have been found to be important in studying real human gaze trajectories, especially during realistic search tasks. In this section we make use of the theoretical foundations of this model as a sampling theory, and identify the functional advantages of each pattern in an abstract sampling process within neural computation. To quantify the role each signature provides, we define a set of information-theoretic metrics for search efficiency and, for each metric in turn, describe how each of the three patterns effects it.
\subsection*{Information gain}\label{subsec:information_gain}
When considering the efficiency advantages of strategic sampling in a visual search task, the central quantity under study is the information gain, $\mathrm{IG}(t)$. To quantify informational across an abstract search landscape, we take inspiration from Bayesian inference and sampling methods. In particular, the link between the explored dynamic potential to a target-belief distribution in a search task in \cref{eq:boltzmann-gibbs} provides a natural place to define information gained in the target search.% The adaptive dynamics of our model when viewed as a sampling  closely resemble well-tempered metadynamics in Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling \cite{tiwary_time-independent_2015}. Similar techniques have been used to model visual search in discrete time by \textcite{bujia_modeling_2022}, where the equivalent target distribution is the likelihood distribution for a search goal, and information is tracked only at the goal position. In \cref{sec:dynamical-information_adaptation} we expanded and unified these approaches by analysing a continuous-time dynamical-information kernel. By tracking the posterior target distribution after kernel deposition at every location in the visual scene, we model global information acquisition across an evolving landscape of belief.

A natural first step in comparing information gathered about a target position through time across a target is the cross-entropy of the posterior to the prior,
\begin{equation}
	H(\pi_t, \pi_0) = -\int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \pi_0(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
however, this cross-entropy alone is of limited utility. Cross entropy will conflate the internal concentration of belief within the posterior (its self-entropy, $H(\pi_t)$) with the net information gained relative to the prior. To decouple these contributions we subtract the posterior entropy to obtain the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_t \parallel \pi_0)$, which gives the formal definition of information gain up to time $t$:
\begin{align}
	IG(t) &= H(\pi_t, \pi_0) - H(\pi_t) \nonumber \\
	&= -\int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \pi_0(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} + \int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} \nonumber \\
	&= \int \pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \log \left( \frac{\pi_t(\mathbf{x})}{\pi_0(\mathbf{x})} \right) d\mathbf{x} \nonumber \\
	&\equiv D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_t \parallel \pi_0). \label{eq:information_gain}
\end{align}
where important properties of the KL divergence is that it is non-negative and asymmetric. In the context of an abstract sampling model, non-negativity reflects the fact that any spatial update from $\pi_0$ represents accumulated evidence, though not necessarily optimal search efficiency. A weak kernel, a slow informative timescale $\tau$, or deposition in low-likelihood areas alters the landscape only marginally and results in slower growth in $\mathrm{IG}$. The information content of the search trajectory is therefore entirely captured by $\mathrm{IG}$, which quantifies the global information acquired as the observer's belief landscape evolves from $\pi_0$ to $\pi_t$. The metrics in the following subsections summarise different aspects of the curve $\mathrm{IG}(t)$.

\subsection{Results}

\subsubsection*{Metrics and comparison conditions}\label{subsec:metrics}
We collected statistics on an ensemble of searches on the computational setup described in \cref{sec:model_setup}, with 200 trials for each parameter set examined, across the entire swept range of parameters. We considered two sweeps: $\alpha-\beta$ sweep for $\alpha$ incremented from $1.1$ to $2.0$ in increments of $0.05$ and $\beta$ from $0$ to $1200$ in increments of $100$, and a $\alpha-\theta$ sweep with the same $\alpha$ range, and $\theta$ varied between $5 \times 10^{-5}$ and $1 \times 10^{-3}$ in increments of $5 \times 10^{-5}$

The most important three metrics that capture complementary aspects of performance: the speed of completion as first passage time, the speed of information gathered as information velocity, and the temporal profile of information gain as a shape exponent. The first passage time ($\mathrm{FPT}$) is the time from trial onset to the first fixation on a target, perhaps the clearest metric of success, with units of seconds. The information velocity $\mathrm{IV} = \langle\mathrm{d}\mathrm{IG}/\mathrm{d}t \rangle$ is the mean value of the rate of information gain, with units of inverse seconds. The shape exponent $\mathrm{SE}$ describes the profile of the $\mathrm{IG}$ curve: we normalised time and $\mathrm{IG}$ to $[0,1]$, computed the area $A$ under the normalised curve, and defined $\mathrm{SE} = 1/A - 1$. This will fit the monotonic information curve the exponent of the power law $\mathrm{IG} \propto t^{\mathrm{SE}}$ with equal area. Thus $\mathrm{SE} = 1$ is accumulation at a constant rate, $\mathrm{SE}< 1$ is decelerating information gain, and $\mathrm{SE}>1$ would be an accelerating rate of information gain.

As an analytical reference for $\mathrm{FPT}$, we considered a searcher that fixates points uniformly at random without replacement, one per theta interval. For $K$ targets among $N$ points, the expected number of fixations to the first target is $(N+1)/(K+1)$\footnote{This is found by considering the expected number of failures in sampling from a hypergeometric distribution with total population $N$ and success population $K$. See for instance \cite{Ahlgren2014-gt}}, and so for $\tau = 2\times 10^{-4}$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fpt_baseline}
  \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{FPT}_0] = t_\theta \, \frac{N+1}{K+1} = \qty{6.0919}{\second}
\end{equation}
% For N=256, K=5, t_theta=0.1422 s this evaluates to about 6.1 s -- confirm parameters.
where $t_\theta$ is the mean saccade interval $t_{\theta} =  \qty{142.22}{\second}$. This reference ignores saliency guidance, momentum and spatial structure, and is drawn as a dashed line in the FPT panels. Two further metrics, the Gini coefficient of binned information gain and the information--entropy gradient $\mathrm{IG}_{\mathrm{H}}$, were also computed and are reported in \cref{app:supp_metrics}.
