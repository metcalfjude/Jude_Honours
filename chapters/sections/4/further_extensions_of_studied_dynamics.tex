\section{Further extensions of studied dynamics}\label{sec:further_extensions_of_studied_dynamic}
The three patterns studied in this thesis were introduced to explain gaze, but each is a general mechanism for exploration of a sparse and unknown landscape: nonlocal superdiffusive jumps, nested oscillations that organise local sampling and transitions between regions of interest, and a history-dependent kernel that records information gathered. Nothing in their construction is specific to vision, and the model we have studied can be extended to a parameter space of any dimension. In this section we outline how they might carry over to two settings in machine learning and artificial intelligence, namely sampling from difficult probability distributions and pursuing goals that change in time. These are proposals for future work and are not tested here.

\subsection{Superdiffusive probabilistic sampling}\label{subsec:superdiffusive_probability_sampling}
A central task in Bayesian inference and generative modelling is to draw samples from a distribution $\pi(\mathbf{x}) \propto \exp\{-V(\mathbf{x})\}$ that may be multimodal, with well-separated modes, or heavy-tailed. Standard gradient-based Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, such as overdamped Langevin dynamics, are driven by Brownian noise and so explore locally. The time to leave a mode grows exponentially with the height of the barrier around it (Kramers' law), so samplers can remain trapped in one mode for very long periods, and the weak gradients in heavy tails make the dynamics inefficient there too \cite{[CITE]}. The same problem is solved by the gaze model: a searcher must visit sparse regions of high likelihood across a landscape without being trapped by any of them.

Each pattern studied here addresses part of this problem. First, driving the dynamics with $\alpha$-stable Lévy noise ($\alpha < 2$) replaces local diffusion by a power-law distribution of jump lengths. For L\'evy flights the escape from a well is governed mainly by the distance to the next mode, rather than by the height of the barrier between them \cite{[CITE]}, so jumps between separated modes occur at a rate that does not decay exponentially with the barrier. The theory of fractional neural sampling (FNS) was formulated for exactly this purpose, and was shown to sample efficiently from multimodal distributions \cite{qi_fractional_2022}. Second, the memory--momentum term produces fast local oscillations within a mode, which decorrelate nearby samples and probe the local gradient of $\pi$. Third, the dynamical-informative kernel of \cref{sec:dynamical-information_adaptation} adds to the potential
\begin{equation}
	V_t(\mathbf{x}) = V_0(\mathbf{x}) + \int_0^t k(\mathbf{x}_{t'}) \, \frac{dt'}{\tau},
\end{equation}
which penalises revisited regions. This closely resembles well-tempered metadynamics \cite{tiwary_time-independent_2015}, in which deposited bias fills explored minima and pushes the sampler towards new ones. The theta-rhythmic transitions provide a fourth ingredient: sampling alternates between phases of local exploitation and phases of nonlocal movement within a single process, rather than through a hand-designed mixture of local and global proposals.

\subsection{Dynamical goals}\label{subsec:dynamical_goals}
In the search tasks studied here the goal is fixed: the likelihood landscape $V_0(\mathbf{x})$ is set before the search begins, and only the observer's belief about it changes. Many problems in machine learning and artificial intelligence instead have goals that vary in time. Targets move or appear, rewards shift, and an agent switches between tasks. Handling these requires a search that can both remember what it has explored and abandon that memory when the objective has changed. This is the situation of the visual system in the active-vision loop, in which the observer's goals and the scene change during a search, and it suggests a natural extension of the model to time-dependent landscapes $V_0(\mathbf{x}, t)$.

The kernel provides a mechanism for this. Currently deposited information accumulates for the whole trial, so a revisited region stays penalised. Adding a finite memory, in which the deposited kernel decays at a rate $\gamma$, creates a trade-off controlled by one parameter. Slow decay strongly avoids revisiting, which suits a static goal, while fast decay allows the sampler to return to regions that may have changed, which suits a moving or changing goal. The other patterns would then have clear roles. The theta rhythm gives a clock on which the current goal is re-evaluated, and the local oscillations measure its gradient, in analogy with the tracking of a moving target in smooth pursuit. Superdiffusive jumps provide a way to reacquire a goal after it has moved far away, a classical use of Lévy search for a relocated target \cite{[CITE]}.

This construction connects to existing frameworks. In reinforcement learning, penalising previously visited states is the basis of count-based and novelty-driven exploration \cite{[CITE]}, and the kernel can be read as a continuous-time version of such a bonus. In active inference, behaviour minimises an expected free energy with an epistemic part, the information gained, and a pragmatic part, progress towards a preferred state \cite{NIPS2017_194cf6c2, [CITE]}. In our framework, the potential $V_0$ would carry the pragmatic goal and the information gain $IG$ the epistemic part, so their balance could be tuned through the kernel strength and the memory rate $\gamma$. The same idea applies to sequence models. Fractional neural attention (FNA) replaces dot-product attention by L\'evy diffusion over tokens, so that short- and long-range dependencies arise from a single operator \cite{qu_fractional_2025}. In this setting a dynamical goal corresponds to a query that changes with the task, and a kernel of previously attended positions could discourage redundant attention in long contexts, with the stability index $\alpha$ made adaptive across layers or heads. As with sampling, these connections are hypotheses, and each would require its own benchmark.
