\section{Further extensions of studied dynamics}\label{sec:further_extensions_of_studied_dynamic}
The dynamics studied in this thesis were introduced to explain gaze, but each component is a general mechanism for exploring a sparse, unknown landscape. Nonlocal superdiffusive jumps provide access to distant regions; momentum-driven local oscillations decorrelate samples within a region; a history-dependent kernel records the information gathered and discourages revisiting; and theta-rhythmic transitions organise the sampler's transitions between regions of interest. Nothing in their construction is specific to vision, and the model can be extended to a state space of any dimension. In this section, we outline how these components might carry over to two settings in machine learning and artificial intelligence: sampling from difficult probability distributions and pursuing goals that change in time. These are proposals for future work and are not tested here.

\subsection{Dynamical goals}\label{subsec:dynamical_goals}
In the search task considered in this study, the goal is fixed: the landscape $V_0(\mathbf{x})$ and the target regions are set before the search begins, and only the observer's belief about it changes. Generally, both in machine learning \cite{cranmer_advances_2023, bremer_predicting_2021} and in tasks studied in neuroscience \cite{tan_theta_2024, Gherri2011-wl}, goals will change over time, as targets move, rewards shift, or agents switch tasks. In the context of a visual search, the searcher must then remember where they have looked to avoid wasting samples and account for the fact that old information may be less useful than new information. This motivates time-dependent landscapes $V_0(\mathbf{x}, t)$ that are intrinsically dynamical, rather than merely reflecting changing beliefs about the landscape. The information kernel already provides memory, so including a mechanism for forgetting is simple: let the kernel effect decay back to zero over time. Writing the potential as the current goal $V_0(\mathbf{x}, t)$ plus a kernel term recording the search history $K_t(\mathbf{x})$, together we find the combined dynamics of $V_t(\mathbf{x}) = V_0(\mathbf{x}, t) + K_t(\mathbf{x})$. We then account for forgetting by letting the kernel effect decay at a rate $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
	\partial_t K_t(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{\tau}k(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_t) - \lambda K_t(\mathbf{x}), \qquad K_0(\mathbf{x}) = 0.
\end{equation}
The case $\lambda = 0$ recovers the present model. For $\lambda > 0$, kernel deposition decays exponentially with memory rate $1/\lambda$ in units of time. This introduces two parameters into the kernel dynamics: one ($\tau$) that defines the theta-rhythmic rate of information gathering, and the second ($\lambda$) that defines the decay rate of this information in dynamical tasks. This model can be used to make rigorous predictions about the effect of working memory on theta-scales in behavioural experiments involving dynamical goals, such as the classic experiment requiring tracking a moving pendulum \cite{law_eye-tracking_1973}. Such a discussion of such empirical studies, which could be developed in a biological setting, is provided in \cref{subsec:smooth_pursuit_and_predictions}

\subsection{Superdiffusive probabilistic sampling}\label{subsec:superdiffusive_probability_sampling}
Given the results of this chapter, we find a promising direction for this framework in abstract probabilistic computation beyond its realisation as a model of gaze. A central task in Bayesian inference and generative modelling is to draw samples from a distribution $\pi(\mathbf{x}) \propto \exp[-V(\mathbf{x})]$ that may be multimodal, with well-separated modes, or heavy-tailed \cite{latuszynski_mcmc_2025, betancourt_conceptual_2018}. FNS was developed to address this problem in cortical activity dynamics, as exploring well-separated heavy-tailed distributions is central to neural sampling dynamics (see \cref{subsec:neur_sampling} \cite{qi_fractional_2022}). From the field of abstract probabilistic sampling, standard gradient-based Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are driven by Brownian noise and therefore explore locally. A well-understood shortcoming of these methods is that the time to escape a mode grows exponentially with the height of the surrounding barrier, so samplers can remain trapped in a single mode for very long periods if not tuned correctly \cite{latuszynski_mcmc_2025}. Furthermore, heavy-tailed distributions give small gradients, which slow the exploration, and often fail to constrain standard MCMC algorithms \cite{behme_levy_2026}.

Each component of the model addresses part of this problem. First, $\alpha$-stable Lévy noise ($\alpha < 2$) replaces local diffusive exploration with global superdiffusion. Escape from a mode then depends mainly on the distance to the next mode rather than the barrier height, with jump rates decaying polynomially rather than exponentially \cite{behme_levy_2026, ijcai2018p419}. Second, momentum coupling produces fast local oscillations which, as in Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, decorrelate nearby samples \cite{ijcai2018p419, betancourt_conceptual_2018}. Third, the information kernel of \cref{sec:dynamical-information_adaptation} adds a history-dependent term that penalises revisited regions and sets a slower timescale organising the fast local sampling. It closely resembles metadynamics \cite{tiwary_time-independent_2015} and can be relaxed once a mode has been explored (\cref{subsec:dynamical_goals}). To our knowledge, a sampler with nested timescales has not been studied. It would decouple local sampling from the global exploration that modulates it, without hand-tuned mixtures of local and global proposals.

All of these dynamics produce an efficient, minimally tuned algorithm that requires only a spatial scale defined by the covariance of the explored target to set the noise strength and ensure local harmonic exploration. Developing a superdiffusive sampler is an active area of research in modern MCMC literature \cite{behme_levy_2026, simsekli_fractional_nodate, ijcai2018p419}, and constructing a model based on the present equations of motion (\cref{eq:fns_system}) is a valuable addition to this literature. A full study in this direction would need to consider the following two components of the model: firstly, the information kernel adjusts the sampled distribution, and this would need to be accounted for through, for instance, reweighting as in metadynamics \cite{tiwary_time-independent_2015, bussi_using_2020, barducci_well-tempered_2008}, and secondly, calculation of the full fractional derivative in the gradient drift term may be computationally prohibitive. Such a study is left to further work.
