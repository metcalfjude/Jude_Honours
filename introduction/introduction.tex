\chapter*{Introduction}

% 1. The problem
The brain is among the most complex systems in science, able to efficiently build a rich internal model of the world from a large quantity of perceptual data. Nowhere is this clearer than in vision. In humans, an estimated one in five cortical neurons is devoted to visual processing and control \cite{wandell_visual_2009, andrews_correlated_1997}, yet despite this computational power, the efficiency with which information is processed is extreme: for every \qty{1}{\mega\byte} of information received by the retina, only about \qty{40}{\bit} are consciously processed \cite{ZHAOPING2026103211, kelly1962information, sziklai1956some}. The visual system is therefore highly selective, building a robust and useful representation of the visible world through the high-acuity perception of the fovea, which lies at the centre of visual attention. In maximising the information gathered, the eyes must actively sample the visual environment through dynamics of visual attention, and thus the eyes. The rapid ballistic shifts in visual attention are called saccades, and they organise fixations during which the brain rapidly gathers visual information, before performing the next saccade. Together, they form a sequence of decisions about where to look next, made several times every second during searching to most efficiently build an internal model of a visual scene \cite{erkelens_coordination_2006, engbert_integrated_2011}. Each decision depends on what has already been seen, what is in view, and the goals of the observer. This process of decision-making informing further visual perception is called \textit{active vision}.

% 2. Importance for neuroscience: gaze as an observable
This makes gaze a valuable observable for understanding the brain. Gaze provides a direct, low-dimensional, continuous, and non-invasive proxy for neural attention \cite{kawashima_attentional_2022, spyropoulos_theta_2018, Bruce_2009_Saliency, chen_spatiotemporal_2022, boccignone_probabilistic_2016, hoffman_probabilistic_2006, wang_attention_2022, misic_active_nodate, ni_distributed_2024}, which is often difficult to track otherwise.
% : the evaluation of stimulus salience \cite{Bruce_2009_Saliency, bujia_modeling_2022, huang_salicon_2015, cornia_predicting_2018}, working memory through inhibition of return (IoR) to explored visual regions \cite{ludwig_mechanism_2009, redden_inhibition_2021, van_ede_looking_2026, henderson_oculomotor_2012, klein_visual_2023}, as well as more generally in complex tasks integrating top-down goals \cite{kingstone_inhibition_1999, postle_selective_2006, schroth_eye_2025}, and the intrinsic timescales of neural activity through regions of visual processing and control (importantly the V1 and the superior colliculus)  \cite{tan_theta_2024, kawashima_attentional_2022, zubair_eye_2026, spyropoulos_theta_2018}. Even the low-amplitude movements made during fixation, once dismissed as noise, carry structure reflecting the underlying oculomotor and neural machinery \cite{ko_eye_2016, graham_ocular_2023, spauschus_origin_1999, otero-millan_fixational_2014, ko_eye_2016, ryle_simultaneous_2015, rucci_control_2015}, and are valuable in gathering information during fixation \cite{wu_fixational_2024, greschner_retinal_2002, bowers_effects_2019, roberts_fixational_2013, rucci_temporal_2018, rucci_unsteady_2015, rucci_miniature_2007, li_perceptual_2026}.
Visual search, in which an observer scans a scene for a target with a determined goal, is among the most demanding and informative settings for studying decision-making in the brain. The subject must balance an exploration--exploitation trade-off, deciding when to continue sampling promising regions and when to discover new ones, while avoiding wasteful re-exploration of depleted regions. This must be done efficiently and robustly within the brain, as the cost of neural computation is high and the brain must be trained to perform well across many tasks \cite{Gherri2011-wl, Li2026-oa, Wright1995-ji, harrison_cost_2023, mangun_sustained_1998}. The resulting trajectories which are found to be most efficient are rich and structured, with characteristic spatial statistics \cite{collewijn_significance_2008, morita_eye_2017, papanikolaou_levy_2024}, temporal correlations \cite{chota_rhythmic_2018, han_gamma_2022}, and preferred timescales \cite{spyropoulos_theta_2018, kawashima_attentional_2022, rucci_temporal_2018}. Understanding the mechanisms behind them offers a window not only onto vision, but onto the general strategies the brain uses to explore, sample, and decide under uncertainty \cite{renninger_where_2007, knill_bayesian_2004, orban_neural_2016, delacour_neural_1997, terada_chaotic_2024, qi_fractional_2022, dong_neural_nodate}.% In studying gaze dynamics, we understand an outline the importance of deficiencies in eye-movement patterns associated with a range of neurological and psychiatric conditions \cite{papanikolaou_levy_2024, caldani_saccadic_2017, morita_eye_2017, zhu_diagnosis_2024, law_eye-tracking_1973, lindner_disorders_2005, bittencourt_saccadic_2013, benitezy_eyetracking_1970}. This finding imbues these measurements with a broad value in science, towards informing biomarkers of global brain function that aggregate many regions involved in visual search.

% 4. Importance for physics, abstract sampling and nonequilibrium exploration of a dynamical goal
The rich structure of gaze trajectories now invites a suitably complex physical description. A challenge in approaching this problem has been in combining the near-optimal information-gathering behaviour of gaze with the variability in the particular trajectories taken. In adressing the information-gathering capacity of gaze there has been success in mapping paths which maximise information gathered \cite{Ruarte2024.11.29.626088, najemnik_eye_2008, najemnik_optimal_2005, sims_optimal_2019}, and in addressing the variability of gaze, there has been much success in modelling gaze trajectories through a stochastic process \cite{engbert_integrated_2011, papanikolaou_levy_2024}. In this thesis we provide a well-motivated theory in the validity of such a stochastic model which maps complex adaptive cortical activity as described by the theory of fractional neural sampling (FNS) \cite{qi_fractional_2022, chen_spatiotemporal_2022} onto the dynamics of gaze. % can be modelled as a patterned stochastic process which has been aggregated from a complex adaptive system in the network of neural activity in a critical regime with memory effects \cite{qi_fractional_2022, chen_spatiotemporal_2022}. 
Considering these dynamics abstractly in exploring an informative landscape, we capture the information gathering which drives search tasks generally. By constructing such a model, we are able to ask and propose answers to quantitative questions about gaze dynamics: what are the functional roles of dynamical patterns such as non-Gaussian heavy-tailed fluctuations, adaptive local search strategies, and regular timescales in improving performance during a visual search? 

% 4. Existing approaches: classical, scale-by-scale
Existing work has approached gaze dynamics largely one scale at a time, both in terms of space and time. Here we present a model which can address gaze dynamics across all scales. Studies of large-scale saccades characterise their timing, amplitudes, and kinematics \cite{erkelens_coordination_2006, otero-millan_fixational_2014,  ludwig_mechanism_2009, rucci_control_2015}. At the scale of whole search trajectories, stochastic models describe global strategies with non-Gaussian heavy-tailed exploration \cite{NIPS2017_194cf6c2, blazejczyk_stochastic_2021, lencastre_dynamical_2025}, or else through global information-maximising strategies \cite{cornia_predicting_2018, bremer_predicting_2021, Ruarte2024.11.29.626088, huang_salicon_2015, kummerer_deepgaze_2022, kummerer_understanding_2017, bhandari_modeling_2025, chen_adaptive_2021, cornia_deep_2016, kittler_effective_2026}. The faster- and shorter spatiotemporal scales of fixational eye movements are then treated as a separate subject, through stochastic descriptions of their high-frequency noise statistics \cite{engbert_integrated_2011, vasudevan_stochastic_nodate, kuang_temporal_2012} or through their perceptual role in rapid information-gathering \cite{martinez-conde_role_2004, otero-millan_fixational_2014}, as well as the low-amplitude drifts during fixation \cite{ko_eye_2016, ryle_simultaneous_2015, rucci_control_2015, martinez-conde_role_2004}. The various timescales of attention more broadly, such as theta rhythmicity, form a further literature rooted in neural recordings and behaviour \cite{tan_theta_2024, kawashima_attentional_2022, spyropoulos_theta_2018}. Each account is valuable on its own terms, but they are developed with different formalisms, different data, and often different assumptions about what the eye is doing.

% 6. Existing approaches: machine learning
% Alongside these, the dominant recent approach to modelling human gaze comes from machine learning. A large body of work trains deep networks to predict either gaze trajectories \cite{bhandari_modeling_2025, wu_gitnet_2026, abdelrahman_deep_2026, bremer_predicting_2021, ozdel_gaze-guided_2024, akinyelu_convolutional_2022} or saliency maps indicating where observers are likely to look \cite{kummerer_modeling_2025, cornia_deep_2016, cornia_predicting_2018, kammerer2016deepgaze2, huang_salicon_2015, bujia_modeling_2022}. Most notably, DeepGaze III \cite{kummerer_deepgaze_2022} predicts human fixation locations with high accuracy given the image, the fixation history, and systematic biases in how people search \cite{kummerer_deepgaze_2022, kummerer_modeling_2025, dagostino2025what}. Such models have been used to test hypotheses about search, showing for example that simple low-level contrast is often sufficient to predict fixations \cite{kummerer_understanding_2017}, and that late fixations become dominated by intrinsic gaze biases and decouple from image content \cite{kummerer_deepgaze_2022}. Yet these networks are optimised to predict where the next fixation will fall, not why gaze behaves as it does. Their internal representations are difficult to interpret, they do not readily connect to biological mechanisms, and they say little about the temporal organisation of search as a dynamical process [CITE].

% 5. The gap
Each of these approaches leaves a gap, both between biological realistic accounts and empirical accuracy, and between the fine and coarse scales of perception which should each be captured under a single theory. Saccades and fixations are not independent processes: the regularity, size, and duration of saccades are influenced strongly by information gathered during fixation \cite{erkelens_coordination_2006, collewijn_significance_2008, ROBINSON2022329}. Models built for a single scale cannot capture this coupling, and their parameters are fitted separately and carry no common physical interpretation. As such, one cannot simply combine such theories into a patchwork theory, as this will not accurately describe the neural control of gaze dynamics. The combined large- and small-scale movements are also rarely measured together, so there are few quantitative constraints on how a unified model should behave across scales \cite{naher_primate_saccades}. As a result, we lack a single, biologically motivated model in which the heavy-tailed spatial statistics of search, the rhythmic timing of saccades, memory of explored regions, and the structured dynamics of fixation arise from the same underlying biologically-informed mechanism. As such, a unified mechanistic account of search dynamics, going beyond fixation prediction, remains largely unexplored. Such an account must reconcile the phenomena above while remaining biologically plausible, parsimonious, and capable of generating testable predictions about novel or unexplained aspects of visual search.

% 6. The roadmap of this thesis
In this thesis, we begin in \cref{chap:literature_review} by surveying the relevant literature in studying the rich dynamics of gaze. This includes introducing the broad patterns of gaze trajectories which identify eye movements: in particular their division into jump-like saccades and periods of information gathering called fixations. We then consider the study of diffusive stochastic models which inform this work, followed by the motivating theory of probabilistic computation and neural sampling as driving the information-gathering dynamics of gaze. We then present the model studied throughout this thesis in \cref{chap:a_unifying_model}. We discuss the key dynamical properties present in the model which are found across a large range of spatiotemporal scales: two temporal scales of nested oscillation, and a global spatial superdiffusion. \Cref{chap:empirical_validations} compares these predictions against real human eye-tracking data, finding validation in the significant regularity of saccade timings, superdiffusion across all spatial scales, and the novel interpretation of a high frequency ocular tremor found in human gaze. In \cref{chap:functional_advantages} we present the strength of this model as it provides an understanding of how these dynamical signatures provide insight into patterns of brain function through the map between visual sampling and cortical sampling. These same functional advantages also apply to general probabilistic sampling strategies, such that the efficiency found in visual search can be understood broadly in terms of both a complex dynamical system, and a probabilistic computational system.
